# Where Concepts Come Apart

## Polyfunctionality, Disagreement, and Progress in Philosophy

*A dissertation in philosophy*

---

### Abstract

Philosophy's central problems are notorious for their persistence. Two and a half millennia after Plato's *Republic*, the questions of what knowledge is, whether we act freely, what makes a person the same person over time, and what it is for one thing to cause another remain contested, and survey evidence suggests that the distribution of professional opinion on them barely moves across a decade. Some conclude that philosophy makes no progress; others redefine progress so that it does. This dissertation offers a third response.

It argues that the concepts at the centre of philosophy's persistent problems are *polyfunctional*: a single concept does several jobs in our practices, each job imposes its own application condition, and the conditions coincide extensionally across the range of cases from which the concept is learned. Philosophical problems arise where these conditions come apart. The thought experiment is the instrument by which such divergence cases are constructed in advance of the world delivering them.

On this account the familiar structure of a philosophical problem, a set of individually compelling but jointly inconsistent claims, is explained rather than merely described: each claim articulates one function of the concept, and each is compelling because the concept really does perform that function. Doctrinal positions are prioritisations of functions. The residual disagreement, once factual and merely verbal components have been removed, is a normative disagreement about which function the shared concept should serve in the divergence region. That disagreement is rationally stable for the same reason that reasonable disagreement about the weighting of plural values is stable, and its persistence is therefore not a scandal.

Progress, on this account, consists in the discovery of divergence, the articulation of functions, their disentanglement into distinct concepts, and the relocation of the residual question to the normative domain, where it can be argued as the practical question it is. The account is developed through four case studies (knowledge, free will, personal identity, causation), defended against objections including a reflexive application to the concept of progress itself, and shown to predict the pattern of stability and variance reported in experimental philosophy.

# Chapter 1. Introduction: The Oldest Thought Experiment

## 1.1 A case from the *Republic*

The oldest surviving philosophical thought experiment is small enough to state in a sentence. In the first book of the *Republic*, the elderly Cephalus offers an account of justice: to tell the truth and to pay back what one has received. Socrates replies with a case. A friend, while in his right mind, lends you weapons; he then loses his mind and demands them back. Returning them would be paying back what one has received. It would not be just (Plato, *Republic* 331c).

Nothing in the exchange is obscure, and yet everything that this dissertation is about is already present in it. Cephalus's account was not foolish. In every case he had encountered in a long commercial life, the just thing to do had been to honour one's obligations and to return what was owed. His concept of justice had been learned from those cases, and it worked. Socrates did not refute it by finding an ordinary case it mishandled. He constructed an extraordinary one, in which two things the concept of justice normally does together, honouring obligations and doing good rather than harm, came apart. The case did not show that Cephalus had mislearned the concept. It showed that the concept, as everyone had learned it, was silent, or rather that it spoke with two voices, in a region no one had visited.

The pattern has never changed. When Edmund Gettier constructed a believer with a justified true belief who nevertheless does not seem to know (Gettier 1963), when Harry Frankfurt constructed an agent who is responsible for what he does though he could not have done otherwise (Frankfurt 1969), when Derek Parfit constructed a person who divides into two (Parfit 1971), and when Ned Hall constructed an effect that depends on an event no process connects it to (Hall 2004), each did what Socrates did to Cephalus. Each took a concept that works, located a region of possible cases in which the several things it does come apart, and built a case in that region. And each produced the same result: not a refutation but a fork, a set of claims that had seemed to be one claim, each of which now had to be weighed against the others.

This dissertation is an attempt to take that pattern seriously as a datum about philosophy, to explain it, and to draw from the explanation a conclusion about the two questions that the pattern most obviously raises. Why do philosophy's central disputes persist among intelligent, informed, and honest inquirers? And what, given that persistence, does progress in philosophy consist in?

## 1.2 The scandal

The persistence of philosophical disagreement has been noticed for as long as there have been philosophers to disagree. Sextus Empiricus made *diaphōnia*, undecidable disagreement among the dogmatists, the first of the five modes by which the Pyrrhonist suspends judgement (*Outlines of Pyrrhonism* I.164–169). Kant opened the first *Critique* by describing metaphysics as the battlefield of endless controversies, a discipline that had for centuries been groping about without finding the secure path of a science (*Critique of Pure Reason* Aviii, Bvii, Bxv). Kant took this to be a scandal in something like the literal sense: a stumbling block to the rational self-respect of the discipline, one he elsewhere applied to the fact that philosophy had still to take the existence of external things on faith (Bxxxix n.).

In our own period the observation has been sharpened by data. Chalmers (2015) asks why there is not more progress in philosophy, and his standard for progress is explicit: large collective convergence on the answers to the big questions, of the kind the natural sciences and mathematics have achieved. By that standard, he argues, philosophy has made little progress, and he supports the claim with the results of the 2009 PhilPapers survey of professional philosophers (Bourget and Chalmers 2014), which found nothing approaching consensus on any of thirty central questions. The 2020 repetition of the survey (Bourget and Chalmers 2023) found the distributions strikingly stable. On free will, for example, the proportion accepting or leaning towards compatibilism was reported as 59.1 per cent in 2009 and 59.2 per cent in 2020. A decade of intense work on Frankfurt cases, manipulation arguments, and the semantics of ability claims had moved the profession's collective opinion by a tenth of a percentage point.

Three broad responses have been made to this situation, and it will help to have names for them.

The *pessimist* accepts Chalmers's standard and concludes that philosophy does not progress. Dietrich (2011) argues, bluntly, that there is no progress in philosophy, that the same problems are debated with the same range of positions as in antiquity, and that this is because philosophical problems are not the kind of thing that can be solved. Brennan (2010) draws a sceptical conclusion about the epistemic standing of philosophical belief; Kornblith (2010) presses the same worry from the epistemology of disagreement; Goldberg (2013) asks how philosophy can be defended in the face of what he calls systematic disagreement.

The *revisionist optimist* rejects the standard. Stoljar (2017) argues that philosophy makes progress on a great many of its questions once one distinguishes the "boundary" and "topic" questions actually being asked from the vaguer big questions with which they are conflated, and that a reasonable optimism is warranted. Dellsén, Lawler, and Norton (2022) apply to philosophy the noetic account of scientific progress, on which progress consists in increased understanding rather than in accumulated knowledge or convergent belief, and argue that on that account philosophy progresses. Williamson (2007) insists that philosophy's methods are continuous with those of other disciplines and that the discipline can do better if it holds itself to higher standards. Gutting (2009) documents a body of things philosophers now know.

The *deflationist* denies that the questions were ever the sort of thing that could be answered. In the Wittgensteinian tradition philosophical problems are symptoms of language "on holiday" (Wittgenstein 1953, §38), to be dissolved rather than solved; the real discovery is the one that lets one stop (§133). In the Carnapian tradition the persistent disputes are "external questions", to be settled pragmatically by a choice of framework rather than theoretically by discovery (Carnap 1950b). And in the recent literature on verbal disputes, Chalmers himself (2011) argues that many philosophical disagreements are at least partly verbal and offers a method of elimination for detecting when they are.

Each of these responses captures something. The pessimist is right that the headline questions do not get settled and that this is not for want of effort. The optimist is right that something cumulative happens in philosophy and that the pessimist's standard is imported from disciplines whose questions have a different structure. The deflationist is right that the disputes have a semantic dimension and that the parties often talk past each other. But none of the three, I shall argue, has correctly identified the *mechanism* that produces the phenomenon they are all responding to. Until one has the mechanism, one cannot say whether the persistence is a failure, what would count as success, or what to do next.

## 1.3 The thesis in outline

The mechanism I propose can be stated in five theses. I state them baldly here; the rest of the dissertation is their defence.

**T1 (Polyfunctionality).** The concepts at the centre of philosophy's persistent problems are *polyfunctional*. A single concept does several distinct jobs in our practices. Each job imposes its own application condition on the concept. Over the range of cases from which the concept is learned and in which it is ordinarily used, these conditions coincide in extension, so that the multiplicity is invisible.

**T2 (Divergence).** Persistent philosophical problems arise in the region of possible cases where the application conditions come apart. The characteristic structure of such a problem, a set of individually compelling but jointly inconsistent propositions, is explained by T1: each proposition articulates the application condition of one function, each is compelling because the concept does perform that function, and the set is inconsistent only over the divergence region. The philosophical thought experiment is the instrument by which divergence cases are constructed in advance of the world producing them.

**T3 (Disagreement).** The doctrinal positions in a persistent debate are prioritisations of functions. Once the factual components of the dispute (about which the parties largely agree) and the merely verbal components (which the method of elimination dissolves) are removed, what remains is a normative disagreement about which function the shared concept should serve in the divergence region. This residual disagreement is neither factual in the ordinary sense nor merely verbal. It is a question of what Burgess and Plunkett (2013) call conceptual ethics, and it is rationally stable among competent inquirers for the same reason that reasonable disagreement about the weighting of plural goods is stable: nothing in the concept, as learned from the coincidence range, fixes the weights.

**T4 (Progress).** Progress on a persistent problem consists in four things: locating the divergence region by constructing cases; articulating the functions whose divergence the cases reveal; disentangling the functions into distinct concepts; and relocating the residual question to the normative domain, where it can be pursued as the practical question it is. This progress is cumulative, is agreed across doctrinal divides, and constitutes understanding of how our practices hang together. Convergence on the original headline question is not to be expected, and its absence is not a failure.

**T5 (Reflexivity).** The concept of progress is itself polyfunctional, and the metaphilosophical dispute about whether philosophy progresses is itself a divergence case. The account therefore predicts and explains the debate to which it is a contribution. This is not offered as a proof of the account but as a consistency check that the account passes and that its rivals do not obviously pass.

The five theses stand in a definite order of dependence. T1 is an empirical-cum-conceptual claim about a class of concepts. T2 uses T1 to explain the structure of philosophical problems. T3 uses T2 to explain the structure and stability of philosophical disagreement. T4 uses T2 and T3 to say what progress can and cannot be. T5 turns the account on itself.

## 1.4 What the account explains

An account of philosophy's persistent problems should be judged, like any theory, by what it explains. I list here the phenomena that I take to be the explananda and that I shall return to in §4.7 when assessing the abductive case.

**(E1) Plausibility of each horn.** In a philosophical problem the competing claims are not merely logically possible positions; each strikes reflective people as *obviously* true until it is set against the others. A theory should explain why each horn is compelling, not just why the horns conflict.

**(E2) The indispensability of exotic cases.** Philosophical problems are typically forced by cases that are constructed, rare, or physically impossible: fission, teleportation, counterfactual interveners, barn façades, double prevention. Theories of philosophy that treat these as optional illustrations have not understood their role.

**(E3) Persistence without stagnation.** The headline questions persist, yet the literature is not static: distinctions are introduced, positions are refined, some views are abandoned by nearly everyone (justified true belief as an analysis of knowledge; the unrestricted principle of alternate possibilities). A theory should explain why both are true at once.

**(E4) Convergence on decompositions, divergence on headlines.** Philosophers across doctrinal divides agree on the *distinctions* introduced in a debate (regulative versus guidance control; production versus dependence; identity versus what matters) while continuing to disagree about the headline question the distinctions were meant to address.

**(E5) Stability of the distribution of opinion.** Professional opinion on the headline questions is distributed across the positions in proportions that are stable over time and are not tracking the accumulation of arguments.

**(E6) The pattern of experimental findings.** Where experimental philosophers have measured folk judgements, verdicts on paradigm cases are robust across cultures and demographics, while verdicts on the exotic cases are sensitive to framing, affect, and abstraction (§4.3, §4.7).

**(E7) Why the disputes seem substantive to the parties and verbal to the deflationist.** Both appearances are stable and both are held by thoughtful people; a theory should explain how both can be partly right.

**(E8) The recurrence of the same structure outside philosophy.** Disputes of the same shape arise in law (whether a tomato is a vegetable, whether a corporation is a person), in the sciences (whether Pluto is a planet, whether a patient in irreversible coma is dead), and in ordinary life, and are there resolved by explicit legislation rather than by discovery. A theory of philosophy's problems should say what these have in common with philosophy's and why they, unlike philosophy's, get settled.

I shall argue that T1 to T4 explain all eight, that the pessimist's account explains at most E3 and E5, that the optimist's accounts explain E3 and E4 but not E1, E2, or E6, and that the deflationist explains E7 at the price of leaving E1 and E8 unexplained.

## 1.5 Method and scope

The argument of the dissertation is abductive. I do not claim that T1 is a conceptual truth about philosophy, nor that it can be established a priori. I claim that it is the best explanation of E1 to E8, and I try to show this by developing four case studies in enough detail that the reader can check the fit, by saying what the account predicts and what would refute it (§7.9), and by comparing it with its rivals on the explananda.

The case-study method carries an obvious risk of selection. The four problems I treat at length, knowledge, free will, personal identity, and causation, are among the most discussed in analytic philosophy, and were chosen for that reason: if the account fails there it fails. But I have also tried to indicate (§4.6) how the account extends to truth, liberty, moral rightness, existence, set, and scepticism, and I have tried in §7.8 to be candid about the problems it does not obviously cover. The account is a theory of philosophy's *persistent conceptual aporiai*, in a sense to be made precise in Chapter 2. It is not a theory of everything that has been called philosophy. Questions that are straightforwardly empirical, questions that are straightforwardly mathematical, and questions whose residue is metaphysical rather than normative fall outside it or at its edge, and I say so.

Two further methodological commitments should be declared. First, I treat the historical record of philosophy as evidence. If the account is right, the history of a persistent problem should display a characteristic shape, the progressive articulation and disentanglement of functions, and I shall argue that it does. Second, I treat the findings of experimental philosophy as evidence, with the caveats that the replication record of that literature demands. The account predicts a pattern in those findings, and I shall argue that the pattern is there.

## 1.6 Neighbours

The view developed here has neighbours, and honesty requires that I mark at the outset what I take from each and where I part company. Fuller treatments follow in the relevant chapters.

From Rescher (1985, 2009) I take the structural description of philosophical problems as *aporetic clusters*, sets of individually plausible but jointly inconsistent theses, and the observation that philosophical positions differ in which thesis of the cluster they abandon, a choice governed by a priority ordering. What Rescher's account lacks, I argue in §2.4, is an etiology: an explanation of why such clusters form around certain concepts and not others, why the theses are individually plausible, and why exotic cases are needed to expose the inconsistency. Polyfunctionality supplies it.

From the tradition that runs from Craig (1990) through Williams (2002) to Queloz (2021), Hannon (2019), and Simion and Kelp (2020), I take the idea that concepts have functions, that these functions are anchored in practices and needs, and that they can be recovered by genealogical and functional analysis. I part company in insisting that the concepts in question typically have *several* functions, and in locating philosophical problems precisely at the points where these functions diverge. The internal disputes of function-first epistemology, over whether the point of "knowledge" is to flag informants, to close inquiry, or to license assertion, are, on my account, a small-scale instance of the phenomenon they are trying to theorise.

From Gallie (1956) I take the observation that some concepts are "essentially contested" and that their contestation is internal to their use rather than a sign of confusion. I extend the observation from the appraisive concepts Gallie confined it to (art, democracy, social justice) to descriptive concepts such as knowledge and causation, and I supply the mechanism Gallie's seven conditions describe but do not explain (§3.6).

From Dworkin (1986, 2011) I take the notion of an *interpretive concept*, whose correct application depends on the best account of the point of the practice in which it figures. I part company on two points: I do not assume there is always a uniquely best account, and I do not confine interpretive concepts to the evaluative.

From the literature on conceptual engineering and conceptual ethics (Burgess and Plunkett 2013; Cappelen 2018; Burgess, Cappelen, and Plunkett 2020; Haslanger 2012, 2020; Thomasson 2020) I take the thought that questions about which concepts we should use are substantive normative questions. I add a diagnosis: that the persistent disputes of philosophy *already were* such questions, disguised as descriptive questions about the analysis of a single concept, and that this disguise explains both their persistence and their appearance of substance.

From Chalmers (2011) I take the method of elimination for detecting verbal disputes and the concession that a dispute over a term can be substantive when the term plays a role in a practice. I argue (§5.2) that his own method, correctly applied to the persistent problems, yields not the dissolution of the dispute but its relocation, and that the relocated dispute is exactly the normative one T3 describes.

From Beebee (2018) I take the proposal that the aim of philosophy is the achievement and maintenance of *equilibria* rather than knowledge; my account explains why there are several equilibria and what distinguishes them. From Dellsén, Lawler, and Norton (2022) I take the noetic conception of progress; my account says *what* it is that philosophy comes to understand. And from Scharp (2013) and Eklund (2002) I take the possibility that a concept may be defective at its core; I argue that polyfunctionality is a more general and more charitable diagnosis than inconsistency, and that it explains why the defect went unnoticed.

## 1.7 Plan

Chapter 2 characterises the phenomenon. It says what a persistent problem is, reviews the evidence of persistence, sets out Rescher's aporetic structure, identifies three things that structure leaves unexplained, and argues that the role of constructed cases is the key to explaining them.

Chapter 3 develops the theory of conceptual function that the account requires. It distinguishes role functions from etiological functions, adopts a practice-relative notion of the former, gives three methods for identifying the functions of a concept, explains why a single concept comes to bear several, and introduces the distinction between the *coincidence range* and the *divergence region* on which everything depends. It closes by distinguishing polyfunctionality from ambiguity, vagueness, open texture, family resemblance, cluster concepts, essential contestedness, interpretive concepts, and inconsistent concepts.

Chapter 4 states and defends T1 and T2 through the four case studies, sketches extensions, and assembles the abductive case.

Chapter 5 defends T3. It distinguishes three layers of disagreement in a persistent dispute, argues against the purely verbal diagnosis, argues that the residue is normative, explains why it is stable, draws the consequences for the epistemology of disagreement, and answers the charge of relativism.

Chapter 6 defends T4. It surveys accounts of progress, sets out the disentanglement account, documents the record of disentanglements in the four case studies, shows that disentanglement is sometimes followed by re-unification at a higher level of abstraction, argues that philosophy's construction of divergence cases has anticipatory practical value, and shows how the account reconciles pessimist, optimist, and deflationist.

Chapter 7 defends T5 and answers objections: triviality, realism, conventionalism, circularity, demotion, and the apparent counterexamples of philosophy's convergent successes. It marks the account's limits and states what would refute it.

Chapter 8 concludes with a statement of what, on this account, philosophy is for.
# Chapter 2. The Phenomenon: Aporetic Clusters

## 2.1 What a persistent problem is

Not everything that has been called a philosophical problem is the subject of this dissertation. The account I defend is a theory of a specific class of problems, and the first task is to say which.

I shall call a problem a *persistent conceptual aporia* when it satisfies four conditions.

**(2.1) Longevity under scrutiny.** The problem has been considered by competent inquirers over a long period, with each generation in possession of the arguments of its predecessors. This excludes problems that are merely new and problems that persist only because no one has attended to them.

**(2.2) Stability of the position space.** The range of positions taken on the problem has been stable: the same handful of options recurs, refined but not replaced, and the distribution of informed opinion across them does not trend towards one. This excludes problems on which the profession has in fact converged, whether by solution or by abandonment.

**(2.3) Aporetic presentation.** The problem presents as a set of claims each of which commands wide assent when considered on its own and which are jointly inconsistent. This is Rescher's notion of an aporetic cluster, discussed in §2.3. It excludes problems that are simply hard questions with no particularly plausible candidate answers, such as many questions in the history of philosophy or the interpretation of texts.

**(2.4) Case-dependence.** The inconsistency is exhibited, and was historically discovered, by means of cases: situations, actual or imagined, in which the claims deliver conflicting verdicts. This excludes paradoxes whose inconsistency is exhibited by pure derivation from the claims alone. (Whether the boundary between these is sharp is a question I return to in §4.6, where I argue that the set-theoretic paradoxes, which look like pure derivations, are in fact case-dependent in the relevant sense.)

The problems of knowledge, free will, personal identity, and causation, as they are pursued in analytic philosophy, satisfy all four conditions; so, I shall suggest, do the problems of truth, liberty, moral rightness, and existence. By contrast, several kinds of question that have been called philosophical do not, and it is worth being clear that they are not my subject.

First, there are the questions that have *left* philosophy. Austin's image is apt: philosophy has been the "initial central sun, seminal and tumultuous", which from time to time throws off a portion of itself to take station as a science (Austin 1956, 232). Cosmology, the theory of matter, the psychology of perception, and the theory of computation were once philosophical questions and now are not, because the concepts involved acquired a single dominant function, that of prediction or proof, and with it a decision procedure. Chalmers (2015) counts these as philosophical progress and he is right to, but they are not persistent aporiai; they are aporiai that stopped persisting for a reason the present account will identify (§7.7).

Second, there are questions that are *straightforwardly factual* but hard, such as whether God exists or whether there are abstract objects. The parties to these debates take themselves to be disagreeing about how things are, and I shall not argue that they are wrong to. The account has something to say about *some* of these debates, in particular about existence questions where a distinction between existing and being fundamental has been drawn (§4.6), but as a class they fall outside it.

Third, there are *formal results* that were once philosophical questions, such as whether every truth of arithmetic is provable. These were settled by proof, and their settlement is agreed. They are not persistent.

Fourth, there are questions of *interpretation* and *history*, which may be permanently contested but do not present aporetically.

The subject of the dissertation, then, is the class of problems that present as case-dependent aporetic clusters and that have proved stable under long scrutiny. I shall argue that this class is unified by a single mechanism, and that the mechanism explains why its members behave as they do.

## 2.2 The evidence of persistence

That the problems in question are old is not in dispute. The *Theaetetus* ends with the rejection of three accounts of knowledge, including the one that would later be called justified true belief, and offers no fourth (Plato, *Theaetetus* 201d–210a). The ancient debate on fate and responsibility already contained the position that was later called compatibilism: Chrysippus's image of the cylinder, which rolls because it is pushed but rolls as it does because of its own shape, was offered precisely to reconcile universal causation with responsibility (Cicero, *De Fato* 42–43). Locke's chapter on identity and diversity introduced the case of the prince and the cobbler and the distinction between the man and the person, and Butler and Reid replied with objections that are still made (Locke 1690, II.xxvii; Reid 1785, Essay III). Hume gave two definitions of cause and treated them as equivalent (Hume 1748, §VII.ii); Lewis's "Causation" opens by observing that they are not (Lewis 1973, 556).

That the problems are not merely old but *persistent* in the sense of (2.2) is what the survey evidence adds. The PhilPapers survey of 2009 asked professional philosophers in leading departments to state their positions on thirty questions (Bourget and Chalmers 2014). The 2020 survey repeated the thirty and added others (Bourget and Chalmers 2023). Two features of the results matter here.

The first is the absence of consensus. On no question of the thirty did a position command the near-unanimity that is routine for settled questions in the natural sciences. On the questions at the centre of this dissertation, the leading positions attracted pluralities, not majorities, and the minority positions were held by substantial fractions of the profession.

The second is stability. Where the two surveys can be compared, the distributions moved little. The free will figures cited in §1.2 are the sharpest example: 59.1 per cent for compatibilism in 2009, 59.2 per cent in 2020. Such stability across a decade of intensive work is, on the face of it, remarkable. It is not that no arguments were produced. The decade saw the maturation of the manipulation-argument literature, new work on the semantics of ability, a wave of experimental studies of folk judgements, and a good deal of conceptual engineering under the heading of "revisionism". None of it moved the aggregate.

Two cautions are in order. Survey respondents in 2020 were not the same individuals as in 2009, so stability of the aggregate is compatible with individual change. And the survey categories are coarse: "compatibilism" covers positions that differ considerably. But the coarseness cuts in the direction of my thesis rather than against it. If the profession were converging, one would expect the coarse categories to show it. They do not.

There is a third source of evidence, less quantifiable but, to anyone who has worked in these fields, unmistakable: the *phenomenology* of the disputes. The parties to a persistent aporia do not experience one another as ignorant or irrational. They know the arguments. They can state their opponents' positions in terms their opponents accept. They can predict what the reply to any new argument will be, and they are usually right. The experience is not that of an unsolved problem, on which the evidence is not yet in, but of a solved problem whose solution the other side refuses to accept, coupled with the uneasy awareness that the other side has exactly the same experience.

## 2.3 Rescher's structure

The most careful structural description of persistent philosophical problems is Rescher's. In *The Strife of Systems* (1985) and again in *Aporetics* (2009) he argues that philosophical problems characteristically take the form of an *aporetic cluster*: a set of theses each of which is individually plausible, indeed such that we have good reason to accept each, but which are jointly inconsistent. A solution to the problem is then a choice of which thesis to abandon. The several philosophical positions on a problem correspond to the several possible abandonments, and the choice among them is governed by a priority ordering over the theses, an ordering that in turn reflects the cognitive values of the philosopher: the weight given to simplicity, to fidelity to common sense, to systematic power, to economy of ontology, and so on. Philosophical diversity, on Rescher's account, is thus neither a sign of irrationality nor a sign that the questions are empty; it is the natural consequence of rational agents with different priority orderings confronting the same inconsistent set.

The description fits. Consider the four problems that will occupy Chapter 4.

*Free will.* (F1) We are sometimes morally responsible for what we do. (F2) Moral responsibility requires that the agent could have done otherwise. (F3) Our actions are the deterministic consequences of prior states of the world together with the laws of nature. (F4) If determinism is true, no one could ever have done otherwise. The theses are jointly inconsistent. The libertarian rejects (F3); the hard determinist or hard incompatibilist rejects (F1); the classical compatibilist rejects (F4) by reinterpreting "could have done otherwise"; the Frankfurtian compatibilist rejects (F2).

*Knowledge.* (K1) Knowledge is justified true belief. (K2) Smith has a justified true belief that the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket. (K3) Smith does not know it. Here the profession has in fact converged: nearly everyone rejects (K1). But the successor cluster, formed when (K1) is replaced by any of its proposed repairs, does not converge, as §4.2 shows.

*Personal identity.* (P1) Identity is a one–one relation: nothing can be identical to two distinct things. (P2) Psychological continuity, appropriately caused, is what makes a future person me. (P3) In a case of fission, two future persons are each psychologically continuous with me. (P4) What matters in my survival is that there be a future person who is me. Again inconsistent. The no-branching theorist qualifies (P2); the closest-continuer theorist (Nozick 1981) modifies it differently; the four-dimensionalist (Lewis 1976) reinterprets (P1) by counting two persons all along; Parfit (1984) rejects (P4).

*Causation.* Hall (2004) states the cluster explicitly. (C1) Causation is transitive. (C2) Causation is local: causes are connected to their effects by spatiotemporally continuous processes. (C3) Causation is intrinsic: whether c causes e depends only on the intrinsic character of the process connecting them. (C4) Counterfactual dependence of e on c suffices for c to cause e. (C5) Omissions can be causes. Hall shows that no single relation satisfies all five, and concludes that there are two concepts of causation, production and dependence, which divide the theses between them.

The structural description also fits Chalmers's observation (2015, §6) that philosophy's distinctive results are negative or conditional: it is agreed that (K1) fails, agreed that (F2) and (F4) cannot both be held with (F1) and (F3), agreed that (C1) to (C5) are jointly unsatisfiable. What is not agreed is which thesis to give up. That is exactly what Rescher's picture predicts.

## 2.4 Three things the structure does not explain

Rescher's account is a description, and an accurate one, but it is not yet an explanation. Three questions remain, and I take the failure of the description to answer them as the mark of what a genuine theory of philosophy's problems must do.

**The source of plausibility.** An aporetic cluster is not just any inconsistent set. If I believe that all swans are white and also that there is a black swan in Perth, I have an inconsistent set, but no aporia; I simply give up the first belief. What makes a cluster aporetic is that *each* member is compelling, so that no abandonment is comfortable. Rescher registers this by requiring that the theses be "individually plausible", but plausibility is a fact to be explained, not a primitive. Why is (F2), the principle of alternate possibilities, compelling? Why is (P1), the one–oneness of identity, compelling? Why is (C4), the sufficiency of dependence, compelling? An answer of the form "because we have strong intuitions in their favour" relocates the question. A theory of the aporia should say what it is about these theses, and about our relation to the concepts they concern, that makes them compelling even after we know that they cannot all be true.

**Selectivity.** Aporetic clusters form around some concepts and not others. There is no aporia of the prime number, of the uncle, of the kilometre, or of the chess bishop. There are aporiai of knowledge, cause, person, freedom, justice, truth, meaning, and existence. What distinguishes the two lists? It is not abstractness: "prime number" is abstract. It is not vagueness: "kilometre" is precise and "heap" is vague, yet neither generates a persistent aporia of the kind at issue (the sorites is a paradox of a different structure, as §3.6 argues). It is not importance alone: "uncle" matters to a great deal of social life. Rescher's account, which locates the source of diversity in the philosopher's cognitive values, has no resources to explain why those values are engaged by some concepts and not by others.

**Case-dependence.** The inconsistency of an aporetic cluster is typically not visible on inspection. It has to be *shown*, and it is shown by constructing a case in which the theses deliver conflicting verdicts. Before Gettier, (K1) to (K3) were not an aporetic cluster, because (K2) and (K3) had no referent; the cluster came into being when Gettier constructed Smith. Before Frankfurt, (F2) was not under pressure from within the compatibilist camp; it came under pressure when Frankfurt constructed Black and Jones. Hall's five theses were made inconsistent by cases of late pre-emption, double prevention, and omission, not by any derivation from the theses themselves. This is a striking feature. In mathematics, inconsistency is exhibited by derivation; in empirical science, by observation; in philosophy, by the construction of possible situations. Rescher's account treats the aporetic cluster as given and asks how to resolve it; it does not ask why the cluster had to be constructed, or why the construction typically requires cases that are rare, exotic, or physically impossible.

To these three I add a fourth, which is really a feature of the phenomenology noted in §2.2 and which will matter in Chapter 5: the disputes seem substantive to their parties and often seem verbal to observers, and both appearances survive reflection. Rescher's account sides with the parties. The deflationist sides with the observers. Neither explains why the other appearance is so stable.

## 2.5 The role of cases

I take the third of these questions to be the key to the other two, and I close this chapter by looking more closely at what a philosophical thought experiment does.

The recent epistemology of thought experiments has been concerned mainly with the status of the *verdicts* they elicit: whether these are intuitions with a distinctive evidential standing (Bealer 1998; Chudnoff 2013; Bengson 2015), whether they are ordinary judgements about counterfactuals (Williamson 2007, ch. 6), whether they are used as evidence at all (Cappelen 2012; Deutsch 2015; against them Climenhaga 2018), and whether they are trustworthy given their sensitivity to demographic and framing variables (Weinberg, Nichols, and Stich 2001; Machery 2017; in defence Nagel 2012; Sosa 2007b). These are important questions, but they presuppose an answer to a prior one, which has been comparatively neglected: what is the *case* for, as distinct from the verdict? Why does eliciting a verdict about Smith, or Jones, or the divided person, tell us anything about knowledge, or responsibility, or identity?

The standard answer is that the case is a probe of the concept. We possess the concept of knowledge; we cannot state its application conditions directly; so we apply it to a case and record the result, as one might probe a black box with inputs. The verdict is data about the concept's extension, and an analysis is a hypothesis that fits the data.

This answer is correct as far as it goes, but it omits the feature of the cases that makes them philosophically productive. The cases that generate persistent aporiai are not random probes. They have a very specific design. Each is constructed so that features which ordinarily co-occur are made to come apart. Smith's belief is well-grounded *and* true by luck: in ordinary life, well-grounded beliefs are true because they are well-grounded, and the two features coincide. Jones acts on his own reasons *and* could not have done otherwise: in ordinary life, agents who act on their own reasons are agents who could have done otherwise, and the two coincide. The divided person is psychologically continuous with two future people: in ordinary life psychological continuity is one–one, and continuity and identity coincide. Suzy's rock shatters the bottle *and* the shattering does not depend on her throw, because Billy's rock was on its way: in ordinary life, what produces an effect is what the effect depends on, and the two coincide.

In each case the thought experiment is a *divergence constructor*. It specifies a possible situation in which two or more properties, which are correlated across the range of cases from which the concept was learned, are decorrelated. It then asks whether the concept applies. And the answer it receives is, characteristically, either a split verdict, or a firm verdict that on reflection turns out to have chosen one of the properties over the other.

Once this is seen, the three unexplained features of §2.4 come into focus together. The case has to be exotic *because* it must decorrelate properties that are correlated in the ordinary world; if they were easily decorrelated, ordinary experience would have done it and the concept would already have been refined. Aporiai form around *these* concepts and not others *because* these concepts track several correlated properties at once while "prime number" tracks one; the concept of a prime number cannot be put into a divergence case because there is nothing for its single application condition to diverge from. And each thesis of the cluster is compelling *because* each articulates one of the properties the concept tracks, and the concept does track it: (F2) is compelling because our concept of responsibility does, across the ordinary range, apply only to agents who could have done otherwise, and (F1) and the Frankfurtian rejection of (F2) are compelling because it also applies, across the same range, to agents who act on their own reasons, and in the ordinary range these are the same agents.

To turn these observations into a theory, I need an account of what it is for a concept to "track several properties at once", of why a concept should do so, and of what the properties have to do with the practices in which the concept is used. That is the work of the next chapter, and its central notion is that of a *function*.
# Chapter 3. Concepts and Their Functions

## 3.1 Concepts, terms, practices

The account requires three notions, and I shall try to use each in a way that carries as little theoretical baggage as possible.

By a *concept* I shall mean what is expressed by a general term together with the competence to apply it and the inferential and practical role it plays for those who have that competence. I do not assume that concepts are mental representations, abstract objects, or Fregean senses, and nothing in what follows turns on the choice. Cappelen (2018) has argued that talk of "concepts" in this area is best replaced by talk of terms and their meanings, and readers who share his suspicion may make the substitution throughout. What matters is that there is something, expressed by "knows" or "cause" or "same person", that is shared across a community, learned from cases, and put to work.

By a *practice* I shall mean a pattern of coordinated activity, sustained by its participants, that serves ends they have reason to pursue. Practices in this sense include testimony and inquiry, the holding of one another responsible, the making and keeping of commitments across time, the assignment of liability, the explanation and control of events, and the punishment of wrongdoing. The notion is Rawls's (1955), and I shall use it as he did: as a way of talking about what a set of rules and roles is *for* without yet committing to any view about the rules.

By a *function* I shall mean what a concept contributes to a practice. This is the load-bearing notion, and the rest of this chapter is devoted to it.

## 3.2 Two notions of function

The philosophical literature on functions distinguishes two families of account.

On the *etiological* account (Wright 1973; Millikan 1984, 1989), the function of an item is the effect it has had in the past that explains why items of its kind continue to exist. The function of the heart is to pump blood because past hearts' pumping blood is what explains, through natural selection, why there are hearts now. Applied to concepts, an etiological account says that the function of a concept is the effect its past deployments have had that explains why the concept has been retained and transmitted (Millikan 1984, chs. 1–4; for concepts specifically, Simion and Kelp 2020).

On the *role* or *systemic* account (Cummins 1975), the function of an item is the contribution it makes to some capacity of a system that contains it, relative to an analysis of that capacity into the contributions of parts. The function of the heart is to pump blood because pumping blood is the heart's contribution to the organism's capacity to circulate oxygen. Nothing about history is required; a heart that came into existence by chance would have the same function.

For the purposes of this dissertation I adopt a role account, relativised to practices and anchored in needs, as follows.

**(3.1) Definition.** F is a function of concept C in practice P if and only if (a) deployments of C in P contribute to P's achieving an end E; (b) participants in P have reason to pursue E; and (c) the application conditions of C are shaped by that contribution, in the sense that cases in which applying C serves E are the cases from which C is taught and in which its application is uncontested, and cases in which applying C would defeat E are treated as counterexamples to proposed accounts of C.

Clause (a) makes the function a contribution; clause (b) anchors it in a need, so that not every effect of a concept's deployment is a function of it; clause (c) ties the function to the concept's application conditions, so that the function is not external to the concept but is part of what determines when the concept applies. It is clause (c) that does the work in Chapter 4. A concept's functions, on this definition, are not merely uses to which it is put; they are among the determinants of its extension.

I prefer the role account to the etiological one for three reasons. First, the etiological facts about the history of a concept are almost always inaccessible, and where they can be reconstructed they are often idle: what matters for our present use of "knowledge" is what it does for us, not what it did for the community in which it arose. Second, etiological functions can be lost while the item persists, as with vestigial organs, whereas the functions that matter for the aporiai are ones sustained by present need. Third, and most importantly, the role account makes the normative anchoring explicit through clause (b), and the normative anchoring is what allows the account of Chapter 5 to go through.

That said, nothing I argue is incompatible with an etiological gloss. Typically the role functions of a concept are also etiological functions of it, since a concept that does something we need is a concept we retain; this is the thought behind Queloz's (2021) "pragmatic genealogy", which reconstructs the functions of concepts by asking what needs they answer to and treats the answer as explaining the concept's persistence. I shall draw on that method in §3.3. But I do not require that every function of a concept explain its retention, and I allow that a function may be acquired late, as when a term already in use is adopted by a legal system and shaped to legal ends.

It will help to have a rough typology of functions, though I do not claim it is exhaustive or that the kinds are exclusive. A function may be *tracking*: the concept's contribution is to pick out a property or kind, whatever it turns out to be, so that inquiry into the kind can proceed (the function that Putnam 1975 and Kripke 1980 made central to natural kind terms). It may be *coordinating*: the concept's contribution is to align the behaviour or expectations of participants (the function of "same person" in the practice of returning to the same individual for what is owed). It may be *licensing*: the concept's contribution is to mark the point at which some act is permitted or required (the function of "knows" as the norm of assertion, or of "responsible" as the condition of blame). And it may be *orienting*: the concept's contribution is to direct an attitude (the function of "same person" in directing prudential concern, or of "cause" in directing the search for an intervention). The same concept may have functions of several kinds, and this, I shall argue, is the normal condition of the concepts that generate philosophical problems.

## 3.3 Identifying functions

If functions are to do explanatory work, there must be a way of finding out what the functions of a concept are that does not simply read them off from the philosophical positions one wishes to explain. I offer three methods, which in practice are used together.

**Genealogy.** The state-of-nature method of Craig (1990), developed by Williams (2002) and made systematic by Queloz (2021), asks what concept a community of agents with generic human needs and limitations would have to have, given a specified practical problem. Craig's own answer, for knowledge, is that such a community would need to flag good informants: individuals whose testimony on a question can be relied upon. The method then traces how the concept, once introduced, becomes "objectivised" (Craig's term) as it detaches from the perspective of the particular inquirer and comes to mark a property the informant has regardless of who is asking. The method's strength is that it identifies functions from needs rather than from doctrines. Its limitation, for my purposes, is that it tends to deliver *one* function, the one built into the specification of the practical problem, and to treat the others as later accretions. I use it to identify core functions and then ask what other practices the concept has been drawn into.

**Subtraction.** The second method asks what a community would be unable to do if it lacked the concept, or if the concept's application were restricted so as to exclude a class of cases. Haslanger's (2012) ameliorative question, "what do we want the concept to do?", is a version of this, run in the forward direction. The subtraction method has the advantage of surfacing functions that a genealogy, fixed on the concept's origin, may miss. If we lacked the concept of knowledge, we would be unable to flag informants; but we would also be unable to mark the point at which inquiry may stop, or to state the norm that governs assertion, or to state the condition under which one may act on a proposition. Each of these is something we do with the concept, and each, on subtraction, is revealed as a distinct loss.

**Case-partition.** The third method is the one on which the account most depends, and it is the one that connects the theory of functions to the practice of philosophy. Two candidate functions F and F′ of a concept C are *distinct* if and only if there is a possible case on which the application condition associated with F and that associated with F′ deliver different verdicts. If no such case exists, F and F′ are, for the purposes of the theory, a single function under two descriptions. This gives an extensional criterion for individuating functions and a corresponding method for discovering them: construct cases, and see where the verdicts split.

The case-partition criterion has three consequences worth stating. First, it means that the question "how many functions does C have?" is not to be settled by introspection or by lexicography but by the construction of cases, which is to say by the very activity that philosophers engaged in these problems have always been engaged in. The thought experiment is not only a divergence constructor, as §2.5 argued; it is a function individuator. Second, the criterion is defeasible in one direction: a function that has seemed single may be split by a new case, as "control" was split into regulative and guidance control by Frankfurt's case (§4.3), but two functions that have been shown to diverge cannot be re-merged by any further case. The number of functions attributed to a concept can therefore only grow over the history of a problem, which is one reason the history of a problem has the shape it does. Third, the criterion prevents proliferation: one cannot multiply functions by multiplying descriptions, since descriptions that agree on every possible case count as one.

## 3.4 Why one concept acquires several functions

If concepts can have several functions, and if having several functions is what generates persistent problems, one wants to know why concepts should ever be in this condition. Why does a community not simply have one term per function? I identify four pressures, which act together.

**Correlation in the environment of acquisition.** The properties that serve the several functions of a concept are, across the range of cases actually encountered, correlated, and typically correlated for a reason. A person whose beliefs are safe from error is, in the ordinary run of cases, a person who is a reliable informant, whose beliefs are a good basis for action, and who is creditable for getting things right: safety, reliability, action-worthiness, and creditability come together because all are consequences of competent belief-formation in a cooperative environment. An agent who acts on her own reasons is, in the ordinary run of cases, an agent who could have done otherwise, who is the source of her action, and who is an apt target of resentment or gratitude: these come together because the ordinary exercise of practical reason is one in which alternatives are open and no one else is pulling the strings. The organism I am is, in the ordinary run of cases, the psychological continuant I am, the forensic subject I am, and the object of my prudential concern: these come together because psychology is realised in organisms and organisms do not divide. What produces an effect is, in the ordinary run of cases, what the effect depends on and what we would intervene on to prevent it: these come together because ordinary causal chains are single and uncontested. Given such correlation, a term that tracks any one of the properties tracks the others, and a practice that needs one of the others can adopt the term without cost.

**Lexical economy.** Learning and communication favour fewer terms over more (Zipf 1949). A term that can be borrowed by a new practice at no cost of confusion will be borrowed rather than a new term coined. Homonymy is costly only where it produces misunderstanding, and correlation prevents misunderstanding: so long as the properties coincide, no one is ever misled by the fact that "knows" is doing four jobs.

**Underdetermination of the learned concept.** A learner presented with cases from the range in which the properties coincide cannot tell which property the term tracks. This is the situation Goodman (1955) described with "grue", Quine (1960) with "gavagai", and Kripke (1982) with "quus": finite exposure to cases underdetermines the extension of a term over cases not yet encountered. Ordinarily this is treated as a puzzle about how meaning is possible at all. Here it has a different and positive role. Since the learner cannot tell which correlated property is the one, and since for the learner's purposes it does not matter, the term as learned inherits *all* of the correlated properties as candidate application conditions, and nothing in acquisition selects among them. The learner has acquired a concept that is determinate over the range from which it was learned and multiply determined, which is to say not uniquely determined, beyond it.

There is a suggestive analogy in the machine-learning literature. A classifier trained on data in which two features co-occur learns a mixture of the two and behaves unpredictably on inputs where they come apart; the phenomenon has been called "shortcut learning" (Geirhos et al. 2020). The analogy is imperfect in one instructive respect. When a classifier meets an out-of-distribution input, its output is, from the point of view of the intended task, noise. When a competent human concept-user meets a divergence case, her verdict is not noise. It is firm, and it is firm in a direction that tracks which of the concept's practices the presentation of the case has made salient. That is because the human user does not merely possess a mixture of features; she participates in the practices that the features serve, and can be moved to give one of them precedence. This is the fact that Chapter 5 builds on.

**Institutional bundling.** Finally, distinct institutions, law, science, morality, medicine, and ordinary life, each take up the same term and shape it to their ends. Hart and Honoré (1959) showed in detail how the law's concept of causation is shaped by the requirements of assigning liability, and how it therefore departs from the concept used in scientific explanation while sharing its vocabulary. Locke observed that "person" is "a forensic term, appropriating actions and their merit" (1690, II.xxvii.26), which is to say a term shaped by the practice of holding responsible, while also being the term by which we track ourselves in anticipation. Each institution adds a function to the shared term, and each function is invisible to the others so long as the institutions' cases coincide.

## 3.5 Coincidence and divergence

I can now define the notions on which the account turns.

Let a concept C have functions F₁, …, Fₙ, and let each Fᵢ be associated, by clause (c) of (3.1), with an application condition Aᵢ: a condition on cases that specifies when applying C in that case would serve Fᵢ. Then:

**(3.2) Definition.** The *coincidence range* of C is the set of possible cases on which A₁, …, Aₙ all deliver the same verdict. The *divergence region* of C is the set of possible cases on which at least two of them deliver different verdicts. A *divergence case* is a member of the divergence region.

Five claims about these notions will be relied on.

**(3.3)** The coincidence range of a concept that has been successfully learned and transmitted includes nearly all the cases in the environment of its acquisition. This is a consequence of §3.4: if the application conditions frequently diverged in the cases from which the concept was learned, the learner would notice, and the community would either coin a new term or fix a priority. A concept with several functions can be learned as one concept only because the functions coincide where it is learned.

**(3.4)** Within the coincidence range, the concept's verdicts are determinate and robust. All its functions agree, so no framing or emphasis can shift the verdict; the concept behaves as if it had a single application condition, because for these cases it effectively does.

**(3.5)** Within the divergence region, the concept issues conflicting determinate instructions, one per diverging function. It does not fall silent. This is the point at which polyfunctionality differs from vagueness, as §3.6 explains: a borderline case of "heap" yields no verdict, whereas a divergence case of "knows" yields a "yes" from one function and a "no" from another, each of them firm.

**(3.6)** The verdict a competent user actually gives in a divergence case is determined by which function the presentation of the case has made salient. The same case, differently described, will elicit different verdicts, and the difference will track the practice the description foregrounds. This is the account's central prediction about the psychology of thought experiments, and §4.3 and §4.4 present the evidence that bears on it.

**(3.7)** Divergence cases may be actual or merely possible. The world produces them when circumstances change (the discovery of trans-Neptunian objects larger than Pluto; the invention of the ventilator; the possibility of gestational surrogacy; the incorporation of joint-stock companies), and when it does, the community has to legislate. Philosophy specialises in constructing the merely possible ones in advance. The relation between these two activities is the subject of §6.5.

One conceptual question should be addressed before proceeding. When a concept is polyfunctional in the sense defined, is it *one* concept with several functions and an extension that is undetermined over the divergence region, or is it *several* concepts that happen to share a word? I do not think this question has an answer independent of our purposes, and I propose to treat the two descriptions as descriptions of two states. Before divergence has been exhibited and the functions articulated, the community has one concept, with one word, one competence, and one uncontested practice of application; the several functions are latent. After divergence has been exhibited and the functions articulated, the community is in a position to have several concepts, and whether it does depends on whether it coins terms, adopts subscripts, or continues to use the one word with an explicit understanding of its ambiguity. The passage from the first state to the second is what Chapter 6 calls disentanglement, and it is one of the principal forms that progress in philosophy takes. The philosophical debate over whether, say, there is one concept of knowledge or two (Sosa's animal and reflective knowledge) is a debate about whether to make the passage. It is not a debate that has an answer prior to the decision.

A final notion. Concepts differ in *functional load*: in the number of distinct functions they bear and in the stakes attached to each. "Uncle" bears one function, or several that no case has ever separated, and its stakes are low. "Person" bears at least five, and the stakes include the assignment of legal rights, the direction of medical treatment, and the object of a lifetime's concern. The account predicts that the depth and persistence of a philosophical problem should vary with the functional load of the concept at its centre, and that the concepts on Kant's battlefield should be exactly the high-load ones. I take this to be borne out.

## 3.6 Neighbouring notions

Polyfunctionality has relatives, and the account is best understood by contrast with them. I take them in the order of increasing closeness.

**Ambiguity and homonymy.** "Bank" has two meanings with no coincidence range: no case is both a financial institution and a riverside. Competent speakers are never in doubt which is meant. Polyfunctionality is, if one likes, latent homonymy, in which the two meanings have not been distinguished because they have never been observed to come apart. The difference is that homonymy is a fact about a word, while polyfunctionality is a fact about the practices a word serves.

**Polysemy.** "Book" applies to a physical object and to a text; "window" to an opening and to what fills it. Polysemy is the closest linguistic relative of polyfunctionality: related senses, systematically connected, often coinciding. Linguists treat polysemy as a semantic fact about lexical items. The present account explains a species of polysemy, that of high-load concepts, by its practical origin, and it makes a prediction that a purely semantic account does not: that the senses will split exactly where the practices' cases diverge.

**Vagueness.** "Heap" has borderline cases in which it issues no determinate verdict; the sorites exploits this. Vagueness is a matter of degree along a dimension; a divergence case is a matter of conflict among criteria. In a borderline case one does not know what to say; in a divergence case one knows two things to say and they conflict. Alston (1964) distinguished degree vagueness from "combinatory vagueness", in which a term is associated with several conditions no one of which is necessary; combinatory vagueness is closer to polyfunctionality, but Alston's account, like the cluster account below, treats the conditions as an unexplained list and treats their coming apart as producing indeterminacy rather than structured conflict.

**Open texture.** Waismann (1945) observed that empirical concepts are not defined for every possible case: we have not said whether a cat that grew to giant size and then revived after death would still be a cat, because nothing in our experience required us to. Open texture is undefinedness for unanticipated cases. Polyfunctionality is not undefinedness but overdefinedness: for the unanticipated case there are too many definitions, not too few, and they conflict. Waismann's phenomenon is real and general; the present account picks out a subclass of it with a specific structure and a specific etiology.

**Family resemblance.** Wittgenstein's observation (1953, §§65–71) that games share no single feature but are linked by overlapping similarities concerns the criteria of membership *within* the range of accepted cases. It is a claim about how the extension of a concept hangs together, not about what the concept is for. Polyfunctionality is a claim about roles, and within the coincidence range there is a shared extension, whatever holds it together. A concept can be both family-resemblant and polyfunctional; the notions are orthogonal.

**Cluster concepts.** The cluster theory of names (Searle 1958; cf. Wittgenstein 1953, §79 on "Moses") and Putnam's (1962) account of "law-cluster" concepts such as "energy" hold that a concept is associated with a cluster of criteria, no one of which is necessary and some weighted majority of which is sufficient. This is the closest structural relative of the present account, and Putnam's discussion of what happens to "energy" when relativity forces the abandonment of one law in the cluster is an account of a scientific disentanglement. The difference is that the cluster theory says nothing about *why* those criteria cluster, and it predicts that when the criteria come apart the concept will fall silent or apply by weighted vote. The present account anchors the criteria in functions, explains their clustering by correlation, and predicts structured conflict rather than silence, with the verdict tracking salience of function.

**Essentially contested concepts.** Gallie (1956) argued that certain concepts, his examples were "art", "democracy", "social justice", "religion", and "the Christian tradition", are such that disputes about their proper use are not resolvable by argument or evidence and are not, for that reason, confused. He gave seven conditions: the concept is appraisive; the achievement it signifies is internally complex; that achievement is variously describable, its components differently weightable; it is open to modification in unforeseen circumstances; each party recognises the others' use as contesting the same concept; the concept derives from an exemplar; and continued contestation sustains or develops the exemplar's achievement. Waldron (2002) has complained that the notion is applied too freely. The present account both vindicates and disciplines Gallie. His "internally complex" and "variously describable" conditions are, in my terms, the claim that the concept has several functions differently weighted; his "openness" is the divergence region; his requirement that the parties recognise a common concept is the coincidence range. What Gallie did not supply is a mechanism, and he restricted the phenomenon to appraisive concepts. I supply the mechanism and lift the restriction: "knowledge" and "cause" are contested in exactly Gallie's way, and the restriction to appraisive concepts was an artefact of the examples he happened to choose. The account also gives Waldron his criterion: a concept is essentially contested if and only if it is polyfunctional, its functions are normatively loaded, and the community faces actual or seriously entertained divergence cases.

**Interpretive concepts.** Dworkin (1986, ch. 2; 2011, ch. 8) distinguished criterial concepts, which are shared by sharing criteria of application, natural-kind concepts, which are shared by pointing at a kind, and interpretive concepts, which are shared by participating in a practice and whose correct application depends on the best account of the *point* of that practice. His examples were justice, liberty, equality, law, and courtesy. My "function" is close to Dworkin's "point", and my divergence region is close to the region where, on his account, the practice's paradigms give out and interpretation begins. I part company on two points. First, Dworkin holds that there is a uniquely best interpretation and therefore a right answer to interpretive questions; I hold that the plurality of points may be irreducible, so that there need be no fact of the matter about which function should dominate, though there may be better and worse answers. Second, Dworkin classifies concepts as interpretive *tout court*; I hold that the same concept is criterial in its coincidence range and interpretive in its divergence region. This explains why Dworkin's critics, who insist that "justice" and "law" have ordinary criteria of application that any competent speaker knows, are right, and why Dworkin, who insists that in hard cases only interpretation will do, is also right.

**Concept and conceptions.** Rawls (1971, §1), following Hart (1961), distinguished the *concept* of justice, a role in social life specified abstractly as the proper balance between competing claims, from the various *conceptions* of justice that fill the role differently. The distinction is a version of the present one in which the concept has been identified with *one* function, adjudication among competing claims, and the conceptions with alternative fillings of it. My account suggests that the concept of justice bears several functions (adjudication, but also desert, need-satisfaction, protection of entitlement, reciprocity) and that the conceptions are prioritisations among them. Rawls's own procedure in the original position can be read as an attempt to derive a priority ordering from a fair choice situation, which is to say as an argument in conceptual ethics.

**Manifest, operative, and target concepts.** Haslanger (2012) distinguishes the manifest concept, the one we take ourselves to be using; the operative concept, the one that in fact governs our application; and the target concept, the one we ought to use. The distinctions are orthogonal to mine but interact with it. A divergence case typically reveals a gap between manifest and operative: the manifest concept of knowledge was "justified true belief", and Gettier's case showed that the operative concept was something else. The target question, what concept we ought to use, is the normative residue that Chapter 5 argues is what remains of a persistent dispute once the rest is cleared away.

**Inconsistent concepts.** Eklund (2002) and Scharp (2013) argue that some concepts, truth being the paradigm, are governed by constitutive principles that are jointly inconsistent: the unrestricted T-schema together with classical logic yields a contradiction from the Liar. This is a structural cousin of the present account, located at the level of principles rather than functions. My account relates to it as follows. The constitutive principles are articulations of functions; the T-schema articulates truth's function as a device of disquotation and generalisation (Quine 1970; Horwich 1990). The inconsistency appears only in the divergence region, which for "true" is the region of semantically closed self-reference where the disquotational function comes apart from the function of marking grounded, assertable content (Kripke 1975 makes groundedness the operative notion). Over its coincidence range, the concept of truth is not defective at all, and this explains what an inconsistency account struggles with: why a concept governed by inconsistent principles should have been serviceable for the whole history of human thought and remain so. Scharp's proposal to replace "true" with a pair of concepts, ascending and descending truth, is on my account an instance of disentanglement, and the account explains why that is the right shape for a solution to take.

The following table summarises the contrasts.

The notion of a *verbal dispute* (Chalmers 2011) is taken up in §5.2, because its proper assessment requires the theory of disagreement developed there. And the *natural kind* model of concepts (Kripke 1980; Putnam 1975; for knowledge, Kornblith 2002) is taken up in §7.3, because it is the basis of the most serious realist objection to the account.
# Chapter 4. The Divergence Thesis

## 4.1 The thesis stated

With the apparatus of Chapter 3 in hand, T1 and T2 can be stated as a set of claims about any persistent conceptual aporia in the sense of §2.1.

**(4.1)** The aporia concerns a polyfunctional concept C, with functions F₁, …, Fₙ (n ≥ 2) that are distinct by the case-partition criterion of §3.3.

**(4.2)** Each thesis of the aporetic cluster is either an articulation of the application condition Aᵢ associated with some function Fᵢ, or a claim about the logical form of C (its arity, its transitivity, its determinacy) that some Fᵢ requires.

**(4.3)** The theses of the cluster are jointly satisfiable over the coincidence range of C and jointly unsatisfiable only over its divergence region.

**(4.4)** The cases that expose the inconsistency are divergence cases: possible situations constructed so that properties correlated in the environment of acquisition are decorrelated.

**(4.5)** The verdicts elicited by divergence cases are prioritisations. A verdict expresses which function the subject gives precedence to under the case's presentation, and it varies with presentation where presentation varies the salience of functions.

**(4.6)** The doctrinal positions on the aporia correspond to standing prioritisations of functions.

I now argue that (4.1) to (4.6) hold for each of four problems. For each I proceed in the same order: the functions of the concept, identified by the methods of §3.3; the coincidence of their application conditions in the ordinary run of cases; the divergence cases that have shaped the debate; the positions as prioritisations; and the fit with the explananda of §1.4. I have tried to keep the exposition of the debates themselves to what is needed for the argument, and I assume a reader who knows them.

## 4.2 Knowledge

**Functions.** Genealogy delivers a first function. Craig (1990) argues that a community with the generic need to acquire true beliefs from others would need to mark those individuals whose say-so on a question can be relied upon; the concept of knowledge, in its "prototypical" form, flags good informants. Call this F₁, the *informant* function. Its application condition is roughly: S is a good source on whether p, which requires that p be true and that S's belief be formed in a way that makes S reliable on such matters, in a way the inquirer can detect.

Subtraction reveals others. Were we to lose the concept, we would lose our standard way of marking the point at which inquiry into a question may properly stop. Kelp (2011) and Kappel (2010) argue that this *inquiry-closing* function, F₂, is central: to know whether p is to be in a position where further inquiry into p is pointless. We would also lose the norm that governs assertion. Williamson (2000, ch. 11) argues that one may assert p only if one knows p; whether or not that is the correct norm, the concept of knowledge is the concept we use to state candidate norms of this kind, and F₃, the *assertion-licensing* function, is a distinct job. Hawthorne and Stanley (2008) and Fantl and McGrath (2009) argue that knowledge is the norm for treating a proposition as a reason for action; F₄, the *action-licensing* function, requires that S's epistemic position be good enough, relative to what is at stake, for S to rely on p. Virtue epistemologists (Sosa 2007a; Greco 2010; Zagzebski 1996) treat knowledge as a cognitive *achievement*, a success creditable to the agent's competence; F₅. Anti-luck epistemologists (Pritchard 2005; Sosa 1999) treat knowledge as belief that is *safe*, that could not easily have been false; F₆. And there is a residual function inherited from the Cartesian tradition, that of marking what is *certain* or indubitable, F₇, which Unger (1975) takes to be the operative one and from which he derives scepticism.

Case-partition confirms that these are distinct: for each pair there is a possible case on which the two deliver different verdicts, and the cases are the ones that constitute the post-Gettier literature.

**Coincidence.** In the ordinary run of cases the seven conditions coincide. A belief formed by a competent perceiver in good conditions, or acquired from a reliable and sincere informant, is safe, is creditable to the believer's competence, marks the believer as a good source, closes inquiry, and licenses both assertion and action; and if it is not certain in the Cartesian sense, nothing in ordinary life turns on that. The correlation is not accidental. All seven are consequences of competent belief-formation in a cooperative environment where sources are honest, faculties work, and the world is not arranged to deceive. That is the environment in which the concept was learned, and it is why the concept could be learned as one.

**Divergence cases.** The history of post-Gettier epistemology is the history of the construction of cases in which the conditions come apart.

Gettier's (1963) cases decorrelate internal justification from safety and from credit. Smith has excellent grounds for believing that the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket; his belief is true; but it is true by luck, since his grounds concerned Jones and it is Smith himself who gets the job. Zagzebski (1994) showed that the decorrelation is structural: for any account on which the justification condition does not entail truth, one can construct a case in which bad luck would have made the justified belief false and good luck makes it true after all. In my terms, whenever a concept of knowledge is fallibilist, its justification-based conditions and its safety-based conditions have a non-empty divergence region.

Goldman's (1976) fake-barn case decorrelates achievement and informant-reliability from safety. Henry, driving through a county in which the locals have erected barn façades, looks at the one real barn and believes it is a barn. His faculties are working; he is a fine informant on barns in general; but he could very easily have been wrong.

Lottery cases (Hawthorne 2004) decorrelate high probability from safety. You have every reason to believe your ticket will lose, and it does, but you could easily have been wrong. Hawthorne's own version sharpens the divergence by adding an action-licensing element: you seem to know you will not be able to afford an African safari this year, which entails that your ticket loses.

DeRose's (1992) bank cases decorrelate action-licensing from the informant and inquiry-closing functions. The same evidence that the bank is open on Saturday suffices for action when little is at stake and seems not to when a great deal is; yet nothing about the speaker's reliability or the state of inquiry has changed.

Sceptical scenarios decorrelate certainty from everything else. A brain in a vat has beliefs that are, in every respect that the other six functions care about in the ordinary sense, as good as ours; only the Cartesian function is not served.

**Verdicts.** The account predicts, by (3.6), that verdicts will be robust where a case falls near the coincidence range or where most functions side together, and unstable where the functions are balanced. The evidence bears this out.

Gettier verdicts are robust. Machery, Stich, Rose, and colleagues (2017) found that participants in Brazil, India, Japan, and the United States reliably denied knowledge in Gettier cases; Nagel, San Juan, and Mar (2013) and Turri (2013) found the same among lay participants in North America. On the present account this is what one should expect. In Smith's case five of the seven functions deliver "no": Smith is not safe, not creditable, not a good informant on *this* proposition, and no one would license assertion or action on grounds that are about the wrong man; only the internal-justification reading of F₁ and the inquiry-closing function, on a lenient reading, side with him. The case is near the edge of the divergence region, and the verdict is correspondingly firm. Early reports of cross-cultural variation in Gettier and related verdicts (Weinberg, Nichols, and Stich 2001) have not replicated (Kim and Yuan 2015; Seyedsayamdost 2015), and the robust finding is now the one to be explained.

Fake-barn verdicts are not robust. Colaço, Buckwalter, Stich, and Machery (2014) found that a substantial proportion of participants attributed knowledge to Henry, and the philosophical literature is divided: Pritchard denies knowledge on grounds of safety, Sosa's later work grants Henry an "animal" knowledge while denying him the reflective knowledge that would require his competence to be reliably exercised in that environment (Sosa 2007a, 2011), and others grant knowledge outright. Here the functions are balanced: achievement, informant-reliability, and action-licensing say "yes"; safety says "no". The split is predicted.

Bank-case verdicts shift with stakes, as the action-licensing function requires and the informant function does not, and the three main positions on the cases, contextualism (Cohen 1988; DeRose 1992), subject-sensitive invariantism (Stanley 2005; Fantl and McGrath 2009), and classical invariantism, are three ways of handling the divergence: the first locates the stakes-sensitivity in the semantics of "knows", the second in the metaphysics of knowledge, the third denies it and explains away the appearance.

**Positions as prioritisations.** Knowledge-first epistemology (Williamson 2000) treats the licensing functions, F₃ and F₄, as primary and refuses to analyse knowledge in terms of anything else; the refusal is intelligible if what one wants from the concept is a norm, since norms need not be decomposable. Virtue epistemology prioritises F₅. Anti-luck epistemology prioritises F₆; Pritchard's (2012) "anti-luck virtue epistemology" is an explicit attempt to keep F₅ and F₆ together in one concept by making both necessary, which is to say an attempt to legislate for the divergence region by conjunction. The function-first tradition (Craig 1990; Hannon 2019) prioritises F₁ and reads the others as derived. Contextualism is the position that the prioritisation itself shifts with conversational context, which is close to a statement of (3.6) in semantic dress. And scepticism is the prioritisation of F₇ over all the rest: Unger's (1975) argument is precisely that "knows" is an absolute term whose application condition is certainty, and that nothing meets it. Fallibilism, which nearly every contemporary epistemologist accepts (Cohen 1988), is the standing decision to demote F₇.

**Fit.** The Gettier episode displays E3 (persistence without stagnation) in miniature: justified true belief was abandoned by nearly everyone, and what replaced it was not a successor analysis but a proliferation of positions, each prioritising a different function among those the case had separated. It displays E4: epistemologists across the doctrinal divide accept the *distinctions*, between justification and safety, between animal and reflective knowledge, between the epistemic position needed for assertion and that needed for action, while disagreeing about the headline question. It displays E6, as just shown. And Kaplan's (1985) argument that "it's not what you know that counts", that the concept of knowledge does no work in inquiry that justification and truth do not do, is, in my terms, the observation that once F₂ is separated from F₁ and F₃, the inquiry-closing function can be served without the word.

## 4.3 Free will

**Functions.** The concept at issue is that of the freedom of will or action that is required for moral responsibility; the aporia is about that, not about freedom in the political or the phenomenological sense. Genealogy here is less developed than for knowledge, but Strawson's (1962) reconstruction of the practice of holding responsible from the reactive attitudes, resentment, gratitude, indignation, and their kin, plays the role that Craig's state of nature plays for knowledge: it identifies the practice the concept serves and asks what the practice requires.

Four functions have been articulated by the debate itself, and case-partition confirms their distinctness.

F₁, *leeway*: the concept marks agents who could have done otherwise, in a sense that connects with the principle that "ought" implies "can", with the fairness of blame, and with the openness of alternatives in deliberation. Its application condition is the existence of accessible alternatives.

F₂, *reasons-responsiveness*: the concept marks agents whose actions issue from a mechanism that recognises and responds to reasons, so that praise and blame can function as reasons to them. Its application condition is what Fischer and Ravizza (1998) call guidance control; Wolf (1990) and Frankfurt (1971) articulate cognate conditions.

F₃, *sourcehood*: the concept marks agents who are the origin of their actions in a way that is not itself the product of factors beyond their control. Kane (1996) calls the condition "ultimate responsibility"; Pereboom (2001) makes it the basis of his source incompatibilism; Strawson's (1994) Basic Argument is that nothing could meet it.

F₄, *reactive aptness*: the concept marks agents towards whom the reactive attitudes are appropriate. Its application condition is given by the internal standards of the practice of holding responsible, and Strawson (1962) argued that these standards neither require nor could be undermined by a general metaphysical thesis.

**Coincidence.** In the normal exercise of adult human agency the four coincide, and they coincide for a reason. An agent who deliberates and acts on her own reasons, in circumstances in which no one is manipulating her, is an agent for whom alternatives were open in every sense that ordinary life recognises, who is as much the source of her action as anyone ever is, and towards whom resentment or gratitude is apt. The correlation is so tight that pre-philosophical thought has no separate words for the four.

**Divergence cases.** The debate has constructed, in sequence, cases that decorrelate each function from the others.

The thesis of determinism, made precise in van Inwagen's (1983) Consequence Argument, decorrelates F₁ from F₂. If our actions are consequences of the remote past and the laws, and if no one has power over either, then no one has power over her actions in the sense that would make alternatives accessible; yet a determined agent may be perfectly reasons-responsive. Classical compatibilism (Moore 1912, ch. 6; Ayer 1954) tried to prevent the divergence by reinterpreting "could have done otherwise" conditionally, so that F₁ would coincide with F₂ after all; the failure of the conditional analysis is generally acknowledged, and the compatibilist response has since taken a different form.

Frankfurt's (1969) case decorrelates F₁ from F₂ directly, without appeal to determinism. Jones decides on his own to do something; Black, who wants him to do it, stands ready to intervene if Jones shows signs of deciding otherwise, but never has to. Jones acts on his own reasons and could not have done otherwise. The dominant verdict is that Jones is responsible, and Frankfurt drew the conclusion that F₁ is not required. The minority response, the "flicker of freedom" and "dilemma" defences (Widerker 1995; Kane 1996), argues that the case cannot be constructed without either leaving Jones a residual alternative or presupposing determinism; in my terms, it denies that the divergence region is reachable. The dispute over whether it is reachable has itself been stable for thirty years.

Manipulation cases decorrelate F₂ from F₃. Pereboom's (2001, ch. 4) four-case argument presents Professor Plum, who kills for reasons and through a reasons-responsive mechanism, but whose reasons-responsive character was installed by neuroscientists, by a programme of childhood conditioning, or, in the fourth case, by ordinary deterministic causation. Mele's (2006, ch. 7) zygote argument makes the same point with a designed zygote. Plum meets every compatibilist condition on F₂ and fails F₃. The compatibilist may take the "hard line" (McKenna 2008), holding that Plum is responsible in every case, or the "soft line", adding a historical condition to distinguish the cases; the incompatibilist holds that the cases are alike and Plum is responsible in none. Each response is a prioritisation of F₂ or F₃, and the dispute has not moved.

The luck objection to libertarianism (van Inwagen 2000; Mele 2006) constructs a divergence case *within* the incompatibilist camp, decorrelating F₁ from F₃: if an undetermined choice is "rolled back" and replayed, it sometimes goes one way and sometimes the other, so that the agent has alternatives but seems not to control which is realised. Leeway and sourcehood, which coincided for the libertarian in ordinary cases, come apart, and libertarians divide over which to save.

**Verdicts.** The experimental literature on free will provides the clearest evidence for (3.6) in any domain. Nahmias, Morris, Nadelhoffer, and Turner (2005) found that when participants were presented with a concrete agent in a deterministic universe, most judged the agent free and responsible. Nichols and Knobe (2007) found that when the question was posed abstractly (whether anyone in a deterministic universe could be responsible), most participants gave the incompatibilist answer, while a concrete, affectively charged case (a man who kills his family) elicited the compatibilist one. Sarkissian and colleagues (2010) found the abstract incompatibilist judgement across participants in the United States, Hong Kong, India, and Colombia. Murray and Nahmias (2014) found that a portion of the incompatibilist judgements were driven by participants' reading determinism as "bypassing" the agent's deliberation, which suggests that the abstract framing had engaged F₃ specifically.

On the present account the pattern is not a puzzle to be explained away but a prediction confirmed. The abstract framing makes the metaphysical functions, F₁ and F₃, salient, and they deliver the incompatibilist verdict. The concrete framing makes the practice function, F₄, salient, and it delivers the compatibilist one. The same participants are not confused; they are giving each function its due when it is foregrounded.

**Positions as prioritisations.** Classical compatibilism tried to hold F₁ and F₂ together by analysis. Frankfurt-style and Fischer–Ravizza compatibilism demotes F₁ and prioritises F₂. Strawsonian compatibilism prioritises F₄ and treats the metaphysical functions as confusions. Source incompatibilism (Pereboom) prioritises F₃; leeway incompatibilism (van Inwagen) prioritises F₁; Kane wants both and constructs an account of "self-forming actions" to secure them together. Hard incompatibilism and Strawson's (1994) impossibilism prioritise F₃ and conclude that nothing satisfies it. Dennett's (1984) *Elbow Room*, whose subtitle is "the varieties of free will worth wanting", is in effect an inventory of functions and an argument that the ones worth wanting are compatibilist; Vargas's (2013) "revisionism" is an explicit proposal to redesign the concept of responsibility around F₂ and F₄ and to abandon the folk commitment to F₁ and F₃. Pereboom's (2014) distinction between responsibility in the "basic desert" sense and in forward-looking senses, and Watson's (1996) and Shoemaker's (2011) distinctions among attributability, answerability, and accountability, are disentanglements of the concept of responsibility that the debate has produced and that all parties now use.

**Fit.** The free will debate displays E5 more sharply than any other: the survey figures of §1.2 show the distribution of professional opinion across the prioritisations to be almost perfectly stable across a decade in which every one of the cases above was intensively discussed. It displays E4: compatibilists and incompatibilists agree that the Frankfurt case separates two things that had been conflated, that the manipulation cases separate two more, and that the luck objection separates two within libertarianism; what they do not agree on is which to keep. It displays E6, as just argued. And it displays E1 with particular clarity: each of the four functions is something our practice of holding responsible really does track, and each thesis of the aporetic cluster of §2.3 is compelling for that reason.

## 4.4 Personal identity

**Functions.** Locke (1690, II.xxvii.26) supplied the genealogy in a sentence: "person" is "a forensic term, appropriating actions and their merit". The concept's core function, F₁, is *forensic reidentification*: to mark the individual who is to be held responsible, rewarded, or repaid later as the same individual who acted, was promised, or lent. Subtraction reveals the rest. F₂, *prudential anticipation*: the concept marks the future individual whose experiences I am to anticipate in the special first-personal way, whose pains I dread and pleasures I look forward to as mine; Parfit (1984) made this function central by asking what "matters" in survival. F₃, *organismic tracking*: the concept marks the continuing human animal, the thing that is born, grows, and dies (Olson 1997). F₄, *characterisation*: the concept marks what is truly *mine* among my traits, commitments, and history, the self of self-understanding; Schechtman (1996) distinguished this "characterisation question" from the "reidentification question" and argued that most of the literature had conflated them. F₅, *agential unity*: the concept marks the unity that deliberation and commitment over time require, the standpoint from which one can make a promise now that binds one later (Korsgaard 1989). And there is a social function, F₆, of *recognition by others*: the concept marks the individual whom others continue to treat as one and the same, in the network of relations that Schechtman (2014) calls a "person life".

**Coincidence.** In every actual human life, with the exceptions to be noted, the six coincide. The animal that was born is the psychological continuant that anticipates, the forensic subject that is held to account, the narrative self that understands itself, the agent that binds itself, and the individual others recognise. They coincide because human psychology is realised in a single human organism, organisms do not divide or fuse, memory is continuous, and the social world tracks bodies. The correlation is so complete that the question "which of these am I?" does not arise.

**Divergence cases.** The exceptions are where the debate lives, and unusually for a philosophical problem several of them are actual.

Locke (1690, II.xxvii.15) constructed the first: the soul of a prince, carrying the prince's consciousness, enters the body of a cobbler. F₁ and F₂ follow the consciousness; F₃ stays with the body. Locke's own treatment of the drunkard who does not remember what he did (§22) is a divergence case between the forensic ideal and legal practice: human law punishes the sober man for what the drunk did, Locke observes, because it "cannot distinguish certainly what is real, what counterfeit", so that F₁ as the law must apply it comes apart from F₁ as it ideally would. Reid's (1785, Essay III, ch. 6) brave officer, who remembers his schoolboy flogging as a young soldier and his soldierly exploit as an old general but not the flogging, exhibits a failure of transitivity that the forensic function cannot tolerate and the psychological criterion, in its simple memory form, entails.

Williams (1970) constructed the case that most directly confirms (3.6). Two people, A and B, are to have their psychological contents exchanged, and one of the resulting persons will be tortured. Described in the third person, as a "body-swap", the case elicits the verdict that A goes where A's psychology goes, and should choose that the B-body person be tortured. Described in the first person, as a sequence of things that will be done to *you*, your memories erased, new ones installed, and then torture, the same case elicits the verdict that you remain with your body throughout, and the torture is yours to fear. Williams took the divergence of verdicts to be the problem. On the present account it is the datum: the third-person description makes F₁ and F₄ salient, and they follow the psychology; the first-person description makes F₂ salient, and prudential anticipation, faced with a continuous body about to be hurt, follows the body.

Parfit's (1971, 1984) fission case decorrelates F₂ from the logical form that F₁ requires. My brain is divided and each half is transplanted into a new body; each resulting person is psychologically continuous with me. Identity is one–one, so I cannot be both. But everything that matters to me in survival, on the prudential understanding, is preserved twice over. Parfit's conclusion, that identity is not what matters, is the explicit disentanglement of F₂ from F₁: what the prudential function tracks is Relation R, psychological connectedness and continuity with any cause, and R is not one–one and need not be determinate. The alternatives, the no-branching clause, Nozick's (1981) closest-continuer schema, and Lewis's (1976) proposal that there were two persons sharing a body all along, are attempts to keep F₁ and F₂ together by adjusting the logical form of identity or the counting of persons.

The actual divergence cases are medical. The ventilator made it possible for a human organism to continue while all brain function had irreversibly ceased, decorrelating F₃ from F₂ and F₄; the Harvard committee's report (1968) and the President's Commission (1981) were acts of conceptual legislation in response, and the dispute between whole-brain, higher-brain, and cardiopulmonary criteria of death is a dispute over which function "death" should track. Dementia decorrelates F₂ and F₄ from F₃ and F₆ over time, and the dispute between Dworkin (1993), who argues that the earlier competent person's "precedent autonomy" should govern the treatment of the later demented one, and Dresser (1995), who argues that the present patient's interests should, is a dispute about whether the person of the advance directive is the person in the bed. Nagel (1971) argued that split-brain patients decorrelate the unity of consciousness from the unity of the person.

**Positions as prioritisations.** Psychological continuity theories prioritise F₁, F₂, and F₄, which is why they dominated for three centuries: those are the functions that ordinary reflection on identity most often engages. Animalism (Olson 1997) prioritises F₃, and Olson's "too many thinkers" argument is in effect the complaint that the psychological theorist has severed the concept from the organism that, in every actual case, is the thinker. Narrative theories (Schechtman 1996) prioritise F₄ and explicitly deny that the characterisation question and the reidentification question have the same answer. Korsgaard (1989) prioritises F₅, arguing against Parfit that the unity of the person is a practical presupposition of agency and not a metaphysical discovery. The "simple" or "further-fact" view (Chisholm 1976; Swinburne in Shoemaker and Swinburne 1984) is the insistence that there is a single fact of identity that all the functions track, a position I return to in §7.3 as the realist rejoinder in its natural habitat. And Parfit's own reductionism is the denial that any such fact is needed: the functions are all there is, and they come apart.

**Fit.** The debate displays E2 with unusual purity: nearly every case that has shaped it is physically impossible or medically extreme, because human organisms do not divide and consciousness does not migrate, and only by imagining that they do can the correlated functions be prised apart. It displays E4: animalists and psychological theorists alike accept Schechtman's two questions and Parfit's distinction between identity and what matters as clarifications, whatever they think of the answers. And it displays E8: the actual divergence cases in medicine were settled, to the extent they have been, by legislation rather than by discovery, and the legislation has the form of a decision about which function to privilege.

## 4.5 Causation

**Functions.** Hume gave two definitions of cause and treated them as equivalent: an object followed by another, "where all the objects similar to the first are followed by objects similar to the second"; "or in other words where, if the first object had not been, the second never had existed" (1748, §VII.ii). They are not equivalent, as Lewis (1973, 556) observed at the opening of the paper that founded the counterfactual tradition, and the gap between them is the oldest documented case of latent polyfunctionality in modern philosophy.

Four functions have been articulated. F₁, *production*: the concept marks the process by which one event brings about another, the mechanism or transfer of energy or "derivativeness" (Anscombe 1971) that connects them; process theories (Salmon 1984; Dowe 2000) make this the whole of causation. F₂, *dependence*: the concept marks what the effect depends on, what would have to have been different for the effect not to occur, and thereby what one would intervene on to prevent or produce it; the counterfactual (Lewis 1973, 1986, 2000) and interventionist (Woodward 2003) traditions make this central, and Menzies and Price (1993) trace it to the agent's need to identify levers. F₃, *inference*: the concept marks what licenses inference from one event to another; Hume's first definition and the regularity tradition (Mackie 1974) articulate it. F₄, *selection*: the concept marks *the* cause among the many conditions without which the effect would not have occurred, the one to which explanation, praise, blame, or liability is to be attached. Mill (1843, III.v.3) noted that ordinary usage selects among conditions on grounds that have nothing to do with their causal contribution; Hart and Honoré (1959) showed in detail how the law's selection is governed by the requirements of assigning responsibility.

**Coincidence.** In an ordinary causal sequence, a single chain, uncontested, with no back-ups and no absences doing work, the four coincide. What produced the effect is what it depended on, is what licenses the inference to it, and is what we single out. That is the environment in which the concept was learned, and the reason the concept has one word.

**Divergence cases.** The literature of the last fifty years is a catalogue of decorrelations, and Hall (2004) drew the general conclusion from them.

*Pre-emption* decorrelates F₁ from F₂. Suzy and Billy each throw a rock at a bottle; Suzy's arrives first and shatters it; Billy's sails through the space where the bottle was. Suzy's throw produced the shattering, but the shattering did not depend on it, since Billy's throw would have done the job. Late pre-emption of this kind, and the "trumping" pre-emption of Schaffer (2000), in which Merlin's spell and Morgana's spell both enchant the prince but the earlier spell takes precedence by law, showed that the simple counterfactual analysis could not track production, and Lewis's (2000) revision to "influence" was an attempt to recover it.

*Double prevention* decorrelates F₂ from F₁ in the opposite direction. Suzy is flying a bombing mission; Enemy would have shot her down; Billy, escorting her, shoots Enemy down first. The bombing depends on Billy's action, and the interventionist would identify Billy's action as something to intervene on to prevent it; but no process connects what Billy did to what Suzy did, and the locality and intrinsicness that production requires are absent.

*Omissions* decorrelate F₄ from F₁ and expose the norm-sensitivity of F₄. My plants died because the gardener failed to water them; they did not die because the Queen failed to water them, though the counterfactual dependence is the same (Beebee 2004; McGrath 2005). Knobe and Fraser (2008) found that when a professor who is forbidden to take pens from the department office and an administrative assistant who is permitted to both take pens, and the last pen is thereby unavailable, participants judge the professor to have caused the problem; Hitchcock and Knobe (2009) argue that normative considerations enter causal selection systematically. The selection function is doing what it is for, tracking responsibility, and it is doing it in a way that the production and dependence functions, which are norm-indifferent, cannot follow.

*Transitivity failures* (Hall 2000; Hitchcock 2001) decorrelate the logical form that F₁ seems to require from the verdicts F₂ delivers: a boulder dislodged towards a hiker causes him to duck, and the ducking causes his survival, but the boulder's fall does not cause his survival.

Hall's (2004) conclusion is that the theses of §2.3, transitivity, locality, intrinsicness, dependence, and the causal efficacy of omissions, are jointly unsatisfiable, and that they partition cleanly: production is transitive, local, and intrinsic, and does not admit omissions; dependence is none of these and does. There are, he concludes, two concepts. Cartwright (2004) reached a more radical version of the same conclusion in a paper whose title is a statement of T1 for this domain: "Causation: One Word, Many Things". Godfrey-Smith (2009) surveys the varieties of causal pluralism that have followed.

**Positions as prioritisations.** Process theories prioritise F₁, and their treatment of omissions and double prevention as not "really" causal is the demotion of F₂ and F₄. Counterfactual and interventionist theories prioritise F₂, and Woodward's insistence that his account is meant for the special sciences and for the identification of levers is an explicit statement of which practice the concept is being designed for. Regularity theories prioritise F₃. Legal theories of causation (Hart and Honoré 1959; Moore 2009) prioritise F₄ and build the norm-sensitivity in. Contrastivism (Schaffer 2005) relocates F₄ into the semantics, holding that causal claims are always implicitly contrastive, so that selection is not a distortion of the concept but part of its logical form; this is the causal analogue of contextualism about knowledge. Russell's (1913) argument that the concept of cause should be eliminated from advanced physics is the judgement that physics has no need of any of the four functions in the form the folk concept supplies them, which is, if correct, a reason for physics to drop the word rather than a discovery about causation.

**Fit.** Causation is the domain in which the field has come closest to accepting T1 explicitly, and I take that to be evidence that the diagnosis is natural rather than imposed. It displays E4 completely: process theorists and counterfactual theorists agree that pre-emption and double prevention separate two relations, and they agree on which theses go with which. It displays E8: legal causation is the selection function institutionalised, and its doctrines of proximate cause are legislation for the divergence region. And it displays E1: each of Hall's five theses is compelling because each articulates something that the concept, in its coincidence range, does.

## 4.6 Beyond the four

The four case studies were chosen because they are central and because the fit can be checked in detail. The account is not confined to them, and I sketch here, more briefly, how it applies elsewhere. I make no claim to have established (4.1) to (4.6) for these further cases; I claim that the shape is recognisably the same.

**Truth.** Tarski (1944) observed that the ordinary concept of truth, governed by the schema "'p' is true if and only if p" and applied in a language that contains its own truth predicate, is inconsistent, and confined his definition to formalised languages for that reason. The functions of "true" include disquotation and generalisation (Quine 1970; Horwich 1990), the marking of the aim of inquiry and belief, the marking of correspondence with how things are, and the licensing of assertion. These coincide except under self-reference. The Liar is a divergence case: a sentence for which the disquotational function delivers a verdict and the groundedness that the other functions presuppose is absent. Kripke's (1975) theory locates the divergence exactly, by identifying the grounded sentences as those for which the functions coincide. Scharp's (2013) replacement of "true" by two concepts is disentanglement; Lynch's (2009) functionalism, on which truth is one functional property variously realised, is an attempt to keep one concept by moving up a level of abstraction, a move I discuss in §6.4.

**Liberty.** Berlin's (1958) distinction between negative liberty, the absence of interference, and positive liberty, the presence of self-mastery or of the means to act, is a disentanglement of two functions of "free" that coincide for an unimpeded, resourced, self-governing agent and come apart for the impeded rich, the unimpeded destitute, and the addict. MacCallum's (1967) triadic analysis, on which every freedom claim has the form "x is free from y to do z", is a re-unification at a higher level (§6.4).

**Moral rightness.** Ross (1930) proposed that "right" answers to several *prima facie* duties, fidelity, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, and the rest, no one of which is overriding, and that they coincide in ordinary cases and conflict in hard ones. The trolley cases (Foot 1967; Thomson 1976, 1985) are divergence constructors: they decorrelate the harm-minimising function of "wrong" from its constraint-marking function, and the doctrine of double effect and its successors are attempts to legislate for the region. The stability of the consequentialist–deontological dispute and the sensitivity of trolley verdicts to framing are what the account predicts.

**Existence.** Carnap's (1950b) distinction between internal and external questions, Thomasson's (2015) "easy" ontology, on which existence questions are settled by the application conditions of the relevant sortal, and Sider's (2011) insistence that the interesting ontological questions concern what is *fundamental* or "carves at the joints", can be read as identifying two functions of "exists": the quantificational and inferential function, which Thomasson takes to exhaust the concept, and the function of marking what is basic in reality, which Sider takes to be what ontology was always after. Hirsch's (2011) quantifier variance is the claim that the divergence region here is wholly verbal. Van Inwagen's (1990) Special Composition Question, pursued through cases of gluing, fusing, and living together, is a series of divergence constructions for "object", and Schaffer's (2009) proposal to shift ontology from existence to grounding is a disentanglement.

**Set.** Russell's paradox (Russell 1903, ch. 10) is usually presented as a pure derivation, but it is a divergence case in the sense of (2.4): the naive concept of set has the function of providing an object for every predicate's extension (comprehension) and the function of providing objects that can themselves be members (eligibility for membership), and these coincide for every set anyone had occasion to consider before Russell constructed the set of all sets that are not members of themselves. The iterative conception (Boolos 1971) disentangles them by legislating that membership-eligibility takes precedence, and the convergence of mathematical practice on ZFC, in contrast with the non-convergence of philosophy, is explained in §7.7.

**Scepticism.** The Cartesian sceptic exploits the divergence between the certainty function of "knows" and its other six; fallibilism is the community's standing decision in the divergence region; and the sceptic's residual claim, that the word should be reserved for certainty, is a claim in conceptual ethics, which is how Unger (1975) in effect presents it.

**Punishment.** Rawls (1955) distinguished the justification of the *practice* of punishment, which he took to be utilitarian, from the justification of a *particular* punishment within the practice, which he took to be retributive, and argued that the classical dispute between utilitarians and retributivists conflated the two; Hart (1968) made the same distinction between the "general justifying aim" of punishment and its "distribution". These are disentanglements of the functions of "punishment", and the stability of the retributivist–consequentialist dispute in the divergence region, where the two justifications diverge (punishing the innocent for deterrent effect; refraining from useful punishment on grounds of desert), is what the account predicts.

## 4.7 The abductive case

I return to the explananda of §1.4 and assess the fit.

**E1 (plausibility of each horn).** Each thesis of an aporetic cluster is compelling because it articulates a function the concept in fact performs, and reflective users of the concept can feel it perform that function in the coincidence range. The principle of alternate possibilities is compelling because our concept of responsibility does, across ordinary cases, apply only where alternatives were open. The one–oneness of identity is compelling because the forensic function requires it. The sufficiency of dependence is compelling because the practice of finding levers requires it. No rival account explains this. The pessimist takes plausibility as given; the deflationist treats it as an illusion, which does not explain why the illusion has the specific content it does; the optimist has no account of it at all.

**E2 (indispensability of exotic cases).** The cases must be exotic because they must decorrelate properties that are correlated in the ordinary world for good reasons, and only unusual, extreme, or physically impossible situations do that. The account explains not only that the cases are exotic but *how*: each is constructed by holding one correlated property fixed and removing another. No rival account explains why philosophy should need such cases at all.

**E3 (persistence without stagnation).** The headline question persists because it is a question about the concept's application in the divergence region, where the concept's functions conflict and nothing in the concept settles the conflict. The literature does not stagnate because each new case individuates a new function, each new function is a new distinction, and distinctions accumulate. The pessimist explains persistence but not the accumulation; the optimist explains the accumulation but must redefine progress to account for the persistence; the present account explains both from a single mechanism.

**E4 (convergence on decompositions, divergence on headlines).** The parties agree on the distinctions because the distinctions are individuations of functions by case-partition, and case-partition is a matter of fact: either the case separates the verdicts or it does not. They disagree on the headline because the headline asks which function should govern, and that is not a matter the cases settle. This is the pattern Chalmers (2015) noticed when he observed that philosophy's agreed results are negative and conditional, and it is here given a reason.

**E5 (stability of the distribution).** The distribution of opinion is stable because it is a distribution of prioritisations, prioritisations reflect the weights individuals give to the practices the functions serve, and the accumulation of arguments about cases does not change those weights; it only makes clearer what the choice is. That the free will figures should be as stable as they are is, on any rival account, an embarrassment. On this one it is what should happen.

**E6 (pattern of experimental findings).** Paradigm and near-paradigm verdicts are robust because in the coincidence range and at its edge the functions agree or nearly all agree. Verdicts in balanced divergence cases are unstable and framing-sensitive because the functions conflict and presentation selects among them. The free will findings of Nichols and Knobe (2007) and the identity findings of Williams (1970) are direct confirmation of (3.6). Accounts that treat framing effects as noise to be filtered out (Nagel 2012; Sosa 2007b) and accounts that treat them as discrediting the case method (Weinberg, Nichols, and Stich 2001; Machery 2017) both miss what the effects are: measurements of function salience.

**E7 (substantive to the parties, verbal to the observer).** The parties are right that they are disagreeing about something, because which function a load-bearing concept should serve is a substantive question with practical consequences. The observer is right that the parties are, at one level, talking past each other, because at the level of the first-order question ("does Jones have free will?") each is applying a different application condition and there is no shared fact for them to disagree about. Chapter 5 develops this.

**E8 (recurrence outside philosophy).** Pluto, brain death, the tomato, the corporation, the gene, and the species are divergence cases for concepts with several functions, and they are handled by legislation because in those domains a single practice has authority to legislate: the International Astronomical Union, a medical commission, a court, a scientific community with a dominant aim. Philosophy's concepts are load-bearing for several practices at once, none of which has authority over the others, and that is why philosophy's divergence cases do not get legislated. Chapter 7 returns to this.

I conclude that T1 and T2 are well supported. The concepts at the centre of philosophy's persistent problems are polyfunctional; the problems arise where the functions diverge; and the characteristic features of those problems, from the plausibility of each horn to the pattern of experimental findings, follow from that fact. The next two chapters draw out what this implies about disagreement and about progress.
# Chapter 5. Disagreement Reconsidered

## 5.1 Three layers

When a compatibilist and an incompatibilist disagree about whether Jones, in Frankfurt's case, acts freely, what exactly do they disagree about? The question sounds naive, but the persistent aporiai are, I shall argue, precisely the disputes in which it has no naive answer, and the account of Chapter 4 makes it possible to give a careful one.

Any dispute conducted with a polyfunctional concept in its divergence region has three layers.

The **factual layer** comprises the questions about the case whose answers do not depend on which function of the concept one privileges. Is Jones's action produced by a reasons-responsive mechanism? Could Jones have done otherwise, given Black's presence? Was the choice determined? Is Henry's belief safe? Did his perceptual faculty function normally? Is the bottle's shattering counterfactually dependent on Suzy's throw? Is there a process connecting them? These are questions with answers, and in a well-constructed case the answers are stipulated. The parties do not disagree about them. Where a dispute *is* about them, as when compatibilists and incompatibilists disagree about whether determinism is true, it is an ordinary factual dispute and the epistemology of ordinary factual disputes applies.

The **verbal layer** comprises the question whether the concept's term, "free", "knows", "cause", "the same person", is to be applied to the case. Once the factual layer is settled and the functions are articulated, this question can be seen to have no answer independent of a decision about which function's application condition is to govern. The parties disagree here, but their disagreement is, at this layer, a disagreement about the use of a word, and it is at this layer that the deflationist's diagnosis is correct.

The **residual layer** comprises what is left when the factual questions have been answered and the verbal question has been identified as such. I shall argue that something is left, that it is a normative question about which function the shared concept should serve, that it is substantive, and that it is the actual content of the persistent dispute.

The three-layer structure explains E7 of §1.4. The parties are right that they disagree about something substantive, because the residual layer is substantive. The observer is right that they talk past each other, because at the verbal layer they are applying different application conditions to a case about whose facts they agree. Both are right about different layers, and the account of this chapter is an account of the relation between them.

## 5.2 Against the verbal diagnosis

Chalmers (2011) offers a method for detecting verbal disputes. Given a dispute in which a term T figures, bar T and its cognates from the vocabulary and ask whether the parties still disagree about anything they can state. If not, the dispute was verbal; if so, the residual disagreement is the substantive content, and one may proceed to argue about that. Chalmers argues that many philosophical disputes are at least partly verbal in this sense, and he expresses the hope that the method of elimination can clarify them and, in some cases, make them go away.

Let us apply the method to the fake-barn case. Bar "knows" and its cognates. Is Henry's belief safe? The parties agree: no; he could easily have been wrong. Did Henry exercise a reliable perceptual competence? They agree: yes. Would you rely on Henry as a source of information about barns? They agree: yes, in general, though not in this county. Is Henry's belief true and well-founded by ordinary standards? They agree: yes. At the first-order level, nothing remains. The dispute about whether Henry *knows* has dissolved into agreement about everything that can be said without the word.

But now ask: may Henry assert "that is a barn"? Should Henry act on his belief? These questions are not about the word "knows"; they are about the practices of assertion and action. And here the parties may well disagree, because the norms of assertion and action are not stipulated by the case, and because which epistemic condition should govern them is not settled by the facts about Henry. The anti-luck theorist who denies knowledge will typically hold that Henry's assertion is defective, since assertion requires a position from which one could not easily have been wrong. The virtue theorist who grants knowledge will hold that it is not, since Henry did everything an asserter can do. The method of elimination has not dissolved the dispute. It has relocated it: from a dispute about the application of "knows" to a dispute about what should govern a practice that "knows" is used to regulate.

Chalmers anticipates this. He allows that disputes over the use of a term may be worth having when the term is tied to practices with consequences, so that how the term is used affects what people do; a dispute over whether a certain arrangement is a "marriage" is verbal at the first-order level and substantive at the level of legal and social consequences. What he does not do, and what I am doing, is to argue that *all* of philosophy's persistent aporiai are disputes of this kind, and to explain why. They are disputes of this kind because the concepts at their centre are load-bearing for several practices; and the method of elimination, applied to them, always relocates rather than dissolves, because barring the term leaves the practices in place with their conflicting demands.

Plunkett and Sundell (2013; see also Sundell 2011) give the relocated dispute a name and an analysis. A *metalinguistic negotiation* is a dispute in which the parties, by making first-order claims that appear to conflict ("waterboarding is torture"; "waterboarding is not torture"), are in fact negotiating how a word should be used, and are doing so because the word's use has consequences that both parties care about. The dispute is not about the word's current meaning, which may be indeterminate over the case; it is about what its meaning should be. Plunkett and Sundell argue that such disputes are genuine disagreements, expressing conflicting attitudes about a matter both parties regard as important, and that they are substantive whenever the word matters.

The persistent aporiai are metalinguistic negotiations in this sense. "Jones is free" and "Jones is not free", said of the Frankfurt case, are moves in a negotiation over how "free", and with it the practice of holding responsible, should treat agents who act on their own reasons without alternatives. The negotiation is genuine because the practice matters. It is stable because, as §5.4 argues, nothing available to the parties settles it. And it is conducted in the first-order idiom of "analysis", as though it were a dispute about a fact, for a reason the next section gives.

Why do the parties not see their dispute as a negotiation? Two reasons. First, the concept presents itself, in Haslanger's (2012) terms, as a *manifest* concept with a single content; that is how it was learned and how it functions across the coincidence range. The idiom of analysis, of asking what the concept "really" applies to, is the appropriate idiom in the coincidence range, where there is a determinate answer, and the parties carry it into the divergence region, where there is not. Second, the practices at stake are ones the parties are deeply invested in, and a normative disagreement about a practice one is invested in has the phenomenology of a factual disagreement: it feels as though something is *true* about whether Jones is responsible, and not merely to be decided. That phenomenology is, I shall argue in §5.7, not wholly misleading, because normative questions may have answers. But it misleads as to the kind of answer.

## 5.3 The residue is normative

The residual question of a persistent aporia has the following form.

**(5.1)** Given a divergence case in which the application conditions Aᵢ and Aⱼ of concept C conflict, which of them should govern the application of C in cases of this kind, given the practices that C serves and the ends those practices have?

This is a normative question. It asks what *should* be done with a shared instrument. It is not answered by the facts of the case, which are agreed. It is not answered by the concept, which, having been learned in the coincidence range, contains no instruction for the divergence region; the manifest unity of the concept is silent exactly where the question arises. It is answered, if at all, by considerations about the ends the practices serve, the costs of serving one at the expense of another, the feasibility of institutional arrangements, fairness to those affected, and coherence with the rest of the practice. These are the considerations of conceptual ethics (Burgess and Plunkett 2013), and the residual question of a persistent aporia is a question in conceptual ethics.

Two versions of the question should be distinguished. The *internal* version takes the practices as they are and asks which of their ends most needs the concept's service in the region at issue. The *ameliorative* version (Haslanger 2012) asks how the practices themselves should be arranged, and what concept would serve the practices we ought to have. The internal question is the one the parties to a persistent aporia are usually, implicitly, arguing about. The ameliorative question is deeper, and answering the internal question often reveals that it is the ameliorative one that matters.

The clearest confirmation that the residue is normative is that the debates have, in their most self-aware recent phases, turned into ethics. Consider free will. The decomposition of §4.3 leaves the factual questions largely agreed: leeway in the unconditional sense is incompatible with determinism, reasons-responsiveness is compatible, ultimate sourcehood is incompatible and perhaps unsatisfiable. What remains is the question which of these the practice of holding responsible requires, and that is a question about what blame is *for*. It is answered differently by those who take blame to express basic desert (Pereboom 2014, in the negative), by those who take it to be a form of moral communication or address (McKenna 2012; Fricker 2016), by those who take it to express the significance of relationships (Scanlon 2008), and by those who take it to be a forward-looking instrument for shaping conduct (Vargas 2013; Caruso 2021). The compatibilism debate, decomposed, *is* the ethics of blame, and the recent literature has increasingly conducted it as such.

The same is true elsewhere. The knowledge debate, decomposed, becomes a set of questions about the norms of assertion, action, and inquiry: what epistemic position should one be in to tell others things, to rely on a belief when much is at stake, to stop looking? These are questions in the ethics of epistemic practice, and the work of Williamson (2000), Hawthorne and Stanley (2008), and Fantl and McGrath (2009) is, whatever its authors' self-understanding, a contribution to them. The identity debate, decomposed, becomes a set of questions in medical and legal ethics: whose interests govern the treatment of the demented patient, whose consent binds, when a life has ended for the purposes of organ retrieval. The causation debate, decomposed, becomes a set of questions about the aims of the law and of the several sciences: what relation liability should track, what relation an explanation in the special sciences should cite, whether fundamental physics needs any such relation at all. Woodward (2003) is explicit that the interventionist account is designed for a purpose and is to be judged by how well it serves it.

I do not claim that the parties to these debates have all recognised that this is what they are doing. I claim that it is what they are doing, that the account predicts they would end up doing it, and that the trajectory of the debates confirms the prediction.

## 5.4 Why it is stable

Why does the residual disagreement not get settled? Five features of the situation jointly explain its stability.

**Underdetermination by the concept.** Nothing in the concept, as learned from cases in the coincidence range, fixes the relative weights of its functions. The parties cannot appeal to "what the concept really means" because in the divergence region it does not really mean any one thing; the appeal is to a manifest unity that the case has dissolved. Arguments from further cases can locate the divergence more precisely, but they cannot produce a weighting, because the cases are the very thing the weighting is needed to adjudicate.

**Plurality of ends.** The functions serve ends that are genuinely plural and not reducible to a common measure. The practice of holding responsible serves fairness to the agent, protection of others, communication of norms, and the expression of attitudes that are constitutive of certain relationships, and these do not reduce to one another. This is a special case of the plurality of value that Berlin (1958), Nagel (1979), and Williams (1985) have described, and Rawls (1993, Lecture II) listed among the "burdens of judgement" that make reasonable disagreement about fundamental matters the expected condition of free reflection: the difficulty of weighing considerations of different kinds, and the fact that "there are different kinds of normative considerations of different force on both sides of an issue". Where ends are plural, reasonable people weigh them differently, and the difference is not a failure of rationality but its expected output in the absence of a common measure.

**Absence of a legislating authority.** In the domains where divergence cases get settled, one practice has authority over the concept. The International Astronomical Union can legislate what "planet" means for astronomy because astronomy is the practice that uses the word for anything that matters; when it did so in 2006, adopting three criteria and introducing "dwarf planet" for bodies that met two, the dispute ended, not because anyone discovered what Pluto is but because the body with authority decided. The Supreme Court of the United States could rule in *Nix v. Hedden* (1893) that a tomato is a vegetable for tariff purposes, prioritising the culinary function of the word over the botanical for the practice of customs, because the practice of customs has a court. Philosophy's concepts are load-bearing for several practices at once, law, science, morality, medicine, and ordinary life, none of which has authority over the others. No one can legislate what "free" means for all the practices in which responsibility figures, and so no one does.

**Disciplinary home.** The parties characteristically approach the concept from a home practice that they implicitly privilege. Epistemologists trained in the philosophy of science approach "knowledge" as a concept for inquiry and weight the inquiry-closing and informant functions; those trained in ethics approach it as a concept for assertion and action and weight the licensing functions. Free will theorists who are metaphysicians weight leeway and sourcehood; those who are moral psychologists weight reasons-responsiveness and reactive aptness. Training entrenches the weighting and supplies the cases that display it to advantage. The positions are, in this respect, sociologically as well as normatively stable.

**Self-selection.** Finally, philosophers do not arrive at positions on the aporiai by weighing the arguments from a neutral standpoint; they arrive with weightings and find the arguments that express them. This is not a criticism. It is the natural consequence of the fact that the residual question is normative and that normative outlooks precede philosophical training. But it means that the accumulation of arguments about cases cannot be expected to change the distribution of opinion, because arguments about cases sharpen the map and do not change the weights; and arguments about weights, which are arguments in ethics, move slowly and are themselves subject to reasonable disagreement.

Together these explain E5. The distribution of professional opinion on a persistent aporia is a distribution of weightings; weightings are stable under argument about cases; and there is no authority to impose one. The figures of §1.2 are what one should expect.

## 5.5 The epistemology of disagreement

A literature of the last two decades has asked what one should do, epistemically, on discovering that an epistemic peer, someone with the same evidence and comparable competence, disagrees with one. Conciliationists (Feldman 2006; Christensen 2007; Elga 2007) argue that one should move one's credence substantially towards the peer's; steadfast theorists (Kelly 2005; van Inwagen 1996) argue that one may retain one's view. Applied to philosophy (Kornblith 2010; Goldberg 2013; Frances 2014; Ballantyne 2019), conciliationism seems to recommend suspending judgement on nearly every contested philosophical question, since on nearly every such question there are peers on every side.

The present account changes the terms of this discussion in three ways.

First, it separates the layers. On the factual layer of a persistent aporia, philosophers largely agree, and where they disagree, as about determinism, the disagreement is an ordinary one to which the ordinary epistemology applies. On the verbal layer, there is nothing to conciliate about, since there is no fact. The residual layer is where the question of peer disagreement bites, and there the account says something specific.

Second, it changes the object. The conciliationist framework presupposes a shared proposition about which the parties have the same evidence. The residual question is not of that kind. It is a question of how a shared instrument should be used, given plural ends. The parties do not have "evidence" for their weightings in the way one has evidence for a factual belief; they have reasons, drawn from the ends they take the practice to serve, and the reasons on the two sides are, by the burdens of judgement, of different kinds. It is not clear what "moving one's credence towards the peer's" would even mean for a weighting; one can split the difference between two probabilities, but a weighting that gives leeway and reasons-responsiveness equal priority is not a compromise between the two positions but a third position with its own costs.

Third, it changes what suspension would cost. On a factual question, suspension is always available and often wise. On the residual question, suspension is frequently not available, because the practices must act. A court must decide whether the drunkard is to be punished; a hospital must decide whether the patient is dead; a community must decide whether to blame. Where a decision is forced, the epistemically appropriate attitude is not suspension but what I shall call *commitment with awareness*: holding one's weighting, arguing for it in the currency of ends, and recognising the opposing weighting as a weighting rather than as an error. This is close to Beebee's (2018) "equilibrism", on which the aim of philosophy is to achieve and maintain an equilibrium among one's commitments rather than to attain knowledge. The present account explains why there are several equilibria and what distinguishes them: each is a coherent weighting of the functions of a load-bearing concept, and the equilibria differ in which practice they let govern the divergence region.

None of this makes philosophical belief a matter of mere preference. Normative questions may have answers, and a weighting may be better or worse supported by the ends it is meant to serve. But it means that the epistemology of philosophical belief, in the divergence region, is the epistemology of normative belief, not of factual belief, and that the pessimist who applies the latter to it has misidentified the object.

Kornblith (2010) raises a worry that any account of this kind must face. If peer disagreement undermines philosophical belief, it undermines belief in the account itself. The reply is that the account is a claim at the factual and explanatory layer, an abductive hypothesis about the structure of a class of problems, and is answerable to evidence in the ordinary way. Disagreement about it is disagreement in an early-stage inquiry, of the kind that is expected to resolve as evidence accumulates, and the account claims no immunity from it. What the account claims is that the *persistent* disagreements of philosophy are not of this kind, and it gives a reason.

## 5.6 Lewis's menu and van Inwagen's insight

Two remarks by two of the most careful philosophers of the last century can now be read as reports of the structure I have described.

Lewis, introducing the first volume of his *Philosophical Papers*, wrote that the reader in search of knock-down arguments would be disappointed, and that "once the menu of well-worked-out theories is before us, philosophy is a matter of opinion" (1983, x–xi). The remark is usually read as a shrug, or as a confession of the limits of argument. On the present account it is neither; it is accurate. The menu of well-worked-out theories is the map of a concept's functions and of the costs of privileging each; assembling the menu is the work of philosophy that can be done by argument, and it is cumulative and agreed. What remains when the menu is complete is the choice of weighting, and that is a matter of what Lewis called opinion because it is a matter of how one weighs plural ends. Lewis was not saying that philosophy is arbitrary. He was saying that the part of it that argument can settle is the part that assembles the menu.

Van Inwagen, confronting his own disagreement with Lewis about compatibilism, wrote that since he and Lewis had the same evidence and Lewis was at least as able as he, he must suppose that he enjoyed "some sort of incommunicable insight" that Lewis lacked, on pain of admitting that his belief was unjustified (1996). The remark has been much discussed as a statement of the steadfast view. The present account offers a better description of van Inwagen's situation than the one he gave. He and Lewis did not differ in insight. They differed in weighting: van Inwagen gave precedence to leeway, Lewis to the compatibility of ordinary practice with determinism, and each had reasons of the kind appropriate to weightings. The "incommunicability" van Inwagen felt is the mark of a disagreement that is not about a fact accessible to both parties but about the relative importance of ends both recognise. Neither had a defect the other lacked. Both were rational. And the account explains the phenomenology reported in §2.2, of a solved problem the other side refuses to accept: each had solved the problem relative to his weighting, and each experienced the other's weighting as a refusal.

## 5.7 Relativism

The account will be suspected of relativism. If the residual question is answered by a weighting, and weightings vary, does it not follow that whether Jones is free is relative to a weighting, and that anything goes?

Four replies, in increasing order of importance.

First, most of what we say with these concepts is in the coincidence range, where verdicts are determinate, objective, and shared. The account introduces no relativity there. Henry's neighbour, who looked at a barn in a county without façades, knows it is a barn, on every weighting.

Second, within the divergence region, not every weighting is admissible. Weightings are answerable to the ends the practices serve, and some weightings would defeat those ends. A concept of responsibility that gave no weight to reasons-responsiveness would make blame useless as communication and unfair as a response; a concept of legal causation that gave no weight to selection would make liability unbounded; a concept of knowledge that gave no weight to truth would not flag informants. The space of admissible weightings is constrained by the point of the practices, and arguments about which weightings are admissible are arguments with better and worse answers.

Third, the residual question is normative, and whether normative questions have objective answers is a question in metaethics on which the account is neutral. If normative realism is true, then there is a correct weighting of the ends of the practice of blame, and the compatibilism debate is an objective dispute about it, no less objective for being ethical. If normative realism is false, then philosophy's residual disputes are as objective as ethics is, and no less. Either way the account adds no relativity beyond what one already accepts about ethics. What it denies is that the residual question is a *descriptive* question about the concept with a fact of the matter that the concept's content supplies. That denial is not relativism; it is the recognition that the concept's content runs out.

Fourth, the account provides a criterion that the relativist cannot, for distinguishing disputes worth having from disputes not worth having. James's squirrel (1907, Lecture II) is the paradigm of a dispute not worth having. A squirrel on a tree trunk keeps the trunk between itself and a man who circles the tree; does the man go round the squirrel? "Going round" has two functions, tracking passage through the compass points and tracking passage around the object's sides, and they coincide for every ordinary object and diverge for one that keeps turning to face you. So far the case is a divergence case in my sense. But no practice with stakes cares which function governs; nothing anyone does depends on the answer; and so the correct response, which James's campers accepted at once, is to distinguish the senses and drop the question. The persistent aporiai are not like this, and the account says why: their concepts are load-bearing for practices whose ends conflict in the divergence region, and something people do depends on the answer. A dispute is worth having, on this account, exactly when the metalinguistic negotiation has stakes. That is a criterion, and it is one that the parties to philosophy's persistent disputes, who have never been tempted to treat them as squirrel cases, have all along been applying.
# Chapter 6. Progress as Disentanglement

## 6.1 Accounts of progress

The question whether philosophy makes progress cannot be answered without a view about what progress is, and the philosophy of science offers four. On the *epistemic* account (Bird 2007), a field progresses when it accumulates knowledge. On the *semantic* account (Niiniluoto 2014), it progresses when its theories increase in truthlikeness. On the *functional* or problem-solving account (Kuhn 1962; Laudan 1977), it progresses when it solves more problems, or more important ones, than it did. On the *noetic* account (Dellsén 2016), it progresses when it increases understanding, where understanding is a grasp of dependence relations that need not amount to knowledge and need not be accompanied by convergence.

Chalmers (2015) applies to philosophy a criterion that combines the epistemic and the convergent: progress on the big questions would be "large collective convergence" on the truth about them. Stoljar (2017) argues that the big questions are not the ones philosophers actually work on, and that on the "topic" and "boundary" questions they do work on there has been a great deal of progress by any reasonable criterion. Dellsén, Lawler, and Norton (2022) argue that the noetic account, applied to philosophy, yields the verdict that it progresses, and that this is not a redefinition but the application of the account of progress that fits science best.

I take the noetic account to be closest to the truth for philosophy, and I take the present account to supply what it leaves open: a specification of *what* it is that philosophy comes to understand. On the noetic account, understanding is a grasp of dependence relations. The dependence relations that philosophy comes to grasp, on my account, are those among the functions of our load-bearing concepts and the practices they serve: how the concepts hang together, in Sellars's (1962) phrase, and where they come apart. That is a particular kind of understanding, and this chapter argues that it is cumulative, that it is agreed across doctrinal divides, and that it is what the history of a persistent problem has in fact produced.

## 6.2 Disentanglement

T4 says that progress on a persistent aporia consists in four things. I set them out and argue that each is a cognitive achievement with truth conditions.

**(a) Location.** A divergence case is constructed: a possible situation in which the application conditions of two or more functions of the concept deliver different verdicts. That a given case does this is a matter of fact, settled by the case-partition criterion of §3.3. Gettier located a region of the divergence space of "knows" that no one had visited; Frankfurt located one for "free"; Parfit for "same person"; Hall for "cause". Locating a divergence region is the discovery of a modal fact about the concept, that its functions *can* come apart in such-and-such a way, and it is knowledge.

**(b) Articulation.** The functions whose divergence the case reveals are named and their application conditions stated. That a given thesis of the aporetic cluster articulates a given function is again a matter of fact, and one that can be got wrong: it took the literature some years to see that Frankfurt's case separated leeway from reasons-responsiveness rather than, as was first thought, simply refuting the principle of alternate possibilities. Articulation is the achievement of a description of the concept's functional structure, and it is knowledge.

**(c) Disentanglement proper.** Distinct concepts are introduced for the distinct functions, by coining terms ("guidance control"), by subscripting ("knowledge₁" and "knowledge₂", "animal" and "reflective" knowledge), or by explicit disambiguation ("in the sense of production" and "in the sense of dependence"). This is a change in the community's conceptual repertoire, and whether it has been made is a matter of fact about the community. Whether it *should* be made is a further question, but that the resources for making it are now available is an achievement, and one that cannot be undone: by the case-partition criterion, two functions that a case has separated cannot be re-merged by any further case. Disentanglement is cumulative in a way that the position on the headline question is not.

**(d) Relocation.** The residual question, which function the concept should serve in the divergence region, is recognised as normative and handed to the ethics of the relevant practice. That the residue is normative is a claim with truth conditions, defended in Chapter 5; and recognising it is understanding of the structure of one's own inquiry, of the kind that Dellsén's account counts as progress and that any account should.

Each of the four is agreed across doctrinal divides. Compatibilists and incompatibilists agree that Frankfurt's case exists, that it separates leeway from guidance control, that the two can be given distinct names, and, increasingly, that what divides them is a question about the ethics of blame. What they do not agree on is the headline. The account explains why the four are agreed and the headline is not: the four are matters of fact and the headline is a weighting.

Progress by disentanglement changes the *question*. "Is free will compatible with determinism?" is not answered; it is replaced by a set of questions, most of which are answered. Is leeway in the unconditional sense compatible with determinism? No, by the Consequence Argument, which is widely though not universally accepted (Lewis 1981 is the principal dissent, and it turns on a further disentanglement, of two senses of "able to"). Is reasons-responsiveness compatible? Yes, and this is not contested. Is ultimate sourcehood compatible? No, and Strawson (1994) argues it is not satisfiable at all. Which of these does the practice of holding responsible require, or should it require? That is the residue, and it belongs to ethics. To answer a question by decomposing it is a form of answering, and for a question posed with a polyfunctional concept it is the only form available. Biology answered "is a virus alive?" in the same way, by distinguishing metabolism from replication from evolvability and noticing that viruses have some and not others; no one regards that as a failure to answer.

A "solution" to a persistent aporia that answered the headline question *without* decomposition would, on the account, have to be one of two things. It would be an act of conceptual legislation, a decision that one function is to govern, presented as a discovery; or it would be a mistake, an argument that trades on the concept's manifest unity to move from a claim true of one function to a conclusion about another. The history of the persistent problems contains many arguments of the second kind, and the exposure of them is a good part of what the disentanglements have consisted in.

It is agreed across the doctrinal divides. Process theorists and counterfactual theorists of causation use Hall's distinction; animalists and psychological theorists use Schechtman's; deflationists and correspondence theorists use Tarski's. The distinctions are not the property of a school.

It is cumulative in the strong sense noted in §6.2: no entry has been reversed. Functions once separated stay separated. This gives philosophy an arrow of progress that does not depend on convergence about anything but the distinctions themselves.

And it is understanding in the noetic sense. Each entry records a dependence relation: that the verdict of the concept in such-and-such cases depends on which of two things one takes it to be for. To grasp the record is to understand how the concepts of knowledge, freedom, personhood, and causation hang together with the practices of inquiry, blame, self-concern, and explanation, and where they come apart. That is what Sellars said philosophy was for.

Two entries deserve comment because they show that the record extends into what is usually regarded as philosophy's most settled territory. Frege's distinction between sense and reference was forced by a divergence case: the identity statement. "The morning star is the evening star" and "the morning star is the morning star" have the same reference-determining content and different cognitive value, and the concept of meaning that had served both functions without strain in every other kind of sentence came apart on this one. Kripke's separation of the reference-fixing role of a description from its meaning-giving role was forced by another: the standard metre, whose length is fixed by a description ("the length of stick S at t₀") that does not give the meaning of "one metre", since the stick might have been longer. These are the paradigm results of analytic philosophy, and they have the form the account predicts.

## 6.4 Re-unification

Disentanglement is not always the terminus. In several cases the disentangled functions have subsequently been re-unified by a higher-order schema in which the choice of function appears as an explicit parameter.

MacCallum (1967) argued that Berlin's negative and positive liberty were not two concepts but one triadic concept, "x is free from y to do z", in which negative and positive accounts differ in what they admit as values of y and z. Lynch (2009) argued that the several theories of truth, correspondence, coherence, superassertibility, were not rivals but accounts of the different properties that realise a single functional role in different domains of discourse. Schaffer (2005) argued that the selection function of "cause" is not a distortion of the concept but part of its logical form, so that causal claims are always implicitly contrastive and the contrast class is a parameter. Contextualism about knowledge (Cohen 1988; DeRose 1992) makes the stringency of the epistemic standard a parameter set by conversational context. And Fischer and Ravizza (1998) retain the single word "responsibility" while making explicit that it is guidance control, not regulative control, that the word tracks.

Re-unification is progress when it makes the choice of function explicit and parametrises it, so that a user of the concept must say, or can be asked, which value of the parameter is in play. The parameter is exactly the function-selection made visible: the y and z in MacCallum's schema encode which impediments and which achievements the concept is to track; the contrast class in Schaffer's encodes which condition is to be selected; the context in the contextualist's encodes which practice's standard applies. A re-unified concept of this kind does not hide the divergence; it displays it, and it reminds every user that a verdict in the divergence region is a verdict relative to a setting.

Re-unification is not progress when it papers over the divergence. Defining knowledge as "justified true belief that is also safe and also creditable", without saying what the concept does when safety and credit conflict, is a conjunction and not a resolution; it inherits the divergence region of each conjunct. Pritchard's (2012) anti-luck virtue epistemology is more careful than this, since it argues that both conditions are needed and says what work each does, but it does not, on my reading, say what to do about Henry, and the account predicts that it cannot without a decision. The test for a good re-unification is whether the divergence case has an answer under it; the test for a bad one is whether the divergence case has simply been forgotten.

The pattern of disentanglement followed by parametrised re-unification is, I suggest, the characteristic shape of a mature philosophical treatment of a polyfunctional concept. It is also, notably, the shape of the treatment that mathematics gave to "number" (natural, integer, rational, real, complex, each with its own function, unified by the algebraic structures they instantiate) and that physics gave to "mass" (rest mass and relativistic mass, separated by special relativity and re-unified as components of the energy–momentum four-vector; see Field 1973 for the argument that Newtonian "mass" partially denoted both).

## 6.5 Anticipation

Philosophy's divergence cases are exotic, and this has always been held against them. What is the use of knowing what we would say about a person who divides, when no one does?

The answer is that the world produces divergence cases, and when it does the community needs a map of the concept's functions and of the costs of privileging each, and needs it quickly.

The ventilator produced one for "death" in the 1960s: a human organism could now continue with all brain function irreversibly gone. The Harvard committee (1968) and the President's Commission (1981) had to decide which function of "death" was to govern, the cessation of integrated organismic function, the cessation of the capacity for consciousness, or the cessation of heartbeat and breathing, and their reports drew on philosophical work about persons and their persistence (Green and Wikler 1980). Assisted reproduction produced several for "mother", "father", and "parent" in the 1980s, separating genetic, gestational, and social parenthood that had coincided in every previous human birth; the Warnock Report (1984) in the United Kingdom, chaired by a philosopher, was the conceptual legislation. The discovery of Eris, larger than Pluto, produced one for "planet" in 2005, and the International Astronomical Union (2006) legislated three criteria and a new term. The joint-stock company produced one for "person" that the law has been legislating for two centuries. Dementia produces one for "same person" in every case of an advance directive (Dworkin 1993; Dresser 1995). Machine learning is producing them now for "agent", "author", "decision", "responsible", and "understand".

In each of these cases the concept's functions had come apart in reality, a decision was required, and the quality of the decision depended on whether the functions had been articulated and the costs of privileging each understood. Where philosophy had done the work in advance, as with death and personhood, the legislation could draw on it. Where it had not, the legislation was made in haste and has been contested since. Philosophy's construction of exotic cases is, on this account, anticipatory: it maps the divergence regions of load-bearing concepts before the world sends us into them. The person who divides is not going to exist. But the patient whose cortex is gone while her brainstem functions does exist, and the concept that must be applied to her is the one whose divergence region Parfit's cases charted.

This gives philosophy a practical value that is independent of whether it converges on anything. A map of a region no one has entered is not made useless by the absence of a consensus about where in it to build. It is made useful by the arrival of the settlers.

## 6.6 Reconciling the parties

I return to the three responses of §1.2.

The **pessimist** is right that the headline questions do not converge and that they will not. Dietrich's (2011) claim that philosophy's problems are "not the kind of thing that can be solved" is, on the present account, correct: they are the kind of thing that can be decomposed and, in the residue, decided, but not solved, because a question posed with a polyfunctional concept about its divergence region has no answer that the concept supplies. Where the pessimist goes wrong is in the inference from non-convergence to non-progress. That inference requires that progress be measured on the headline question, and the account shows that the headline question is the one place where, given the structure of the problem, progress could not show up.

The **optimist** is right that philosophy progresses, and the account gives the optimism specific content. Stoljar's boundary and topic questions are, in many cases, the decomposed questions of §6.2, and the account explains why they are answerable when the big question is not. Dellsén, Lawler, and Norton's understanding is, in the persistent aporiai, understanding of the functional structure of concepts and its relation to practice. Chalmers's own observation that philosophy's agreed results are negative and conditional is the observation that components (a) to (c) of disentanglement are agreed. The optimist has not needed to redefine progress; the optimist has needed to say what progress on a polyfunctional concept could consist in, and the account says.

The **deflationist** is right that the first-order dispute has a verbal layer and that the method of elimination exposes it. Where the deflationist goes wrong is in supposing that the residue is empty, that once the verbal layer is removed there is nothing to argue about and the fly may leave the bottle. There is something to argue about, it is normative, and it has consequences; the Wittgensteinian counsel to stop is good counsel for the squirrel and bad counsel for the patient in the bed. Carnap was closer: the residual question is, as he said of external questions, practical. But "practical" does not mean "arbitrary", and the choice of a framework for the concept of death is not like the choice of a coordinate system.

There is a residual worry, which should be met directly. Is progress by disentanglement really progress *on the problem*, or is it a change of subject? Someone who asked whether we have free will and is told that the question decomposes into three answered questions and one ethical one may feel that her question has been evaded rather than answered.

Three things can be said. First, for a question posed with a polyfunctional concept, decomposition is not an evasion but the only available form of answer; the alternative is legislation disguised as discovery. Second, the questioner's sense that something has been evaded is the sense that the *residue* has not been answered, and it has not; but the residue is a question in ethics, and the questioner is now in a position to ask it as such, which she was not before. Third, the disentanglements are what the practitioners of these fields themselves regard as progress, as any survey of the literature's self-understanding shows; the account does not impose a standard on the field but articulates the one the field already uses.

The scandal, then, is not a scandal. Philosophy's persistent problems persist because they are questions about the divergence regions of concepts that several practices load at once, and no practice has authority to legislate for the rest. What progress on such questions could consist in, philosophy has made, and made cumulatively. What it could not consist in, convergence on the headline, it has not made, and the expectation that it should was a mistake about the kind of question being asked.
# Chapter 7. Reflexivity, Objections, and Limits

## 7.1 The concept of progress

T5 says that the concept of progress is itself polyfunctional and that the metaphilosophical debate about philosophical progress is a divergence case. I now make that out, and then say what the reflexive application does and does not show.

The concept of progress, as applied to an inquiry, has at least five functions, each articulated by one of the accounts surveyed in §6.1. F₁, *accumulation*: the concept marks an inquiry that has more knowledge than it had (Bird 2007). F₂, *understanding*: it marks an inquiry that grasps more dependence relations than it did (Dellsén 2016). F₃, *convergence*: it marks an inquiry whose practitioners increasingly agree (Chalmers 2015). F₄, *problem-solving*: it marks an inquiry that has solved problems it had not solved (Kuhn 1962; Laudan 1977). F₅, *practical yield*: it marks an inquiry whose results have made a difference to what can be done.

In mature natural science these coincide, and they coincide for a reason. What is discovered is understood; what is understood and discovered is agreed, because the methods that produce discovery are public; what is agreed and understood solves the problems that motivated the inquiry; and what solves those problems can be applied. The coincidence is so tight that philosophers of science have been able to debate which function is *the* concept of progress, each side citing cases in which its favoured function is present, without noticing that the cases they cite are cases in which all five are present. That is the signature of a coincidence range.

Philosophy is a divergence case. By F₁, F₂, and F₄, it progresses: the record of §6.3 is accumulated knowledge, is understanding, and is the solution of problems by decomposition. By F₃ it does not: the headline questions do not converge. By F₅ the verdict is mixed and depends on what one counts, as §6.5 argued. The five functions, which coincide in science, come apart in philosophy, and the metaphilosophical dispute is the predicted result. Chalmers weights F₃; Dellsén, Lawler, and Norton weight F₂; Stoljar weights F₁ and F₄; the pessimist, in taking non-convergence to entail non-progress, weights F₃ without argument that F₃ should govern where the functions diverge. The debate has the three-layer structure of §5.1: the parties agree on the facts (what has been established, what is agreed, what is not), disagree at the verbal layer about whether to call it "progress", and disagree at the residual layer about which function of "progress" should govern the evaluation of a discipline whose questions have the structure described in Chapter 4.

What does the reflexive application show? It does not show that the account is true; an account that predicted its own debate could still be wrong. It shows three things. First, that the account is consistent under self-application: it does not, applied to itself, generate a contradiction or a regress, and it does not exempt itself from the structure it attributes to everything else. Second, that the account has content at the reflexive level, since the reflexive prediction could have failed: had the parties to the progress debate shared a single criterion of progress and disagreed about whether philosophy met it, the account would have been embarrassed. Third, that the account's own recommendation, that "progress" for philosophy be understood in the noetic, disentangling way, is a proposal in conceptual ethics and is argued for as one. It is not offered as a discovery that philosophy progresses. It is offered as a decision about what to count, defended by asking what we want the concept of progress to do when we apply it to a discipline, namely to guide the allocation of effort and to evaluate the discipline fairly, and by arguing that F₂ and F₄ serve that end for philosophy while F₃ does not, because F₃ measures the one thing that the structure of the questions makes unattainable.

The rivals do not obviously pass this test. Chalmers's criterion of large collective convergence is a weighting of F₃ presented as a measure, and no argument is given that F₃ should govern in a domain where it comes apart from the others; the intuition that it should is the manifest unity of "progress" carried out of the coincidence range in which it was learned. That is precisely the error the account diagnoses in the first-order aporiai.

## 7.2 Triviality

*Objection.* Every concept can be said to have several functions; the account is therefore unfalsifiable, and "polyfunctional" is a label that can be attached to anything after the fact.

*Reply.* Three things prevent this. First, many concepts are monofunctional or have never been separated by any case: "prime number", "uncle", "kilometre", "even", "bishop" (in chess). These have exactly one application condition, or several that no possible case separates, and they generate no aporiai. The account predicts a correlation between functional load, as defined in §3.5, and the depth and persistence of philosophical problems, and the correlation is there to be checked. A concept used in many *contexts* is not thereby polyfunctional; "even number" is used in arithmetic, in scheduling, and in games, and has one application condition throughout.

Second, the case-partition criterion of §3.3 disciplines the attribution of functions. One cannot multiply functions by multiplying descriptions; two candidate functions count as one unless a possible case separates their verdicts. This makes "how many functions does C have?" a question with an answer that can be got wrong.

Third, the account makes predictions, listed in §7.9, that could fail. A theory that could fail is not unfalsifiable.

## 7.3 Realism

*Objection.* There is a fact about whether Jones is free, whether Henry knows, whether I survive fission, independent of what functions our concepts serve. The account replaces facts with decisions, and that is not analysis but abdication.

The objection comes in three forms, which deserve separate replies.

**The further-fact view.** Butler (1736), Reid (1785), Chisholm (1976), and Swinburne (in Shoemaker and Swinburne 1984) hold that personal identity is a primitive fact, which the criteria of memory and bodily continuity track imperfectly, and analogous "simple" views can be held about the other concepts. On this view the functions are evidence for a fact that lies behind them, and where the functions diverge the fact is still there, merely hidden.

*Reply.* The view must say what fixes the further fact in a divergence case, and it cannot appeal to the criteria, since they diverge. It is therefore a bare posit. What supports the posit is the phenomenology of the concept's manifest unity, the sense that "same person" means one thing. The present account explains that phenomenology without the posit: the concept feels unitary because it is unitary across the coincidence range, where all its functions agree, and the feeling is carried into the divergence region by the mechanism of §3.4. Of two accounts of the same phenomenology, one with a posit and one without, the one without is to be preferred unless the posit does other work. I do not claim that the further-fact view is refuted; I claim that the burden is on it, and that the account shifts the burden by explaining what the view took to be its evidence.

**Natural-kind realism.** Kornblith (2002) argues that knowledge is a natural kind, to be investigated by cognitive ethology rather than by the analysis of our concept, and the Kripke–Putnam semantics of natural-kind terms (Kripke 1980; Putnam 1975) suggests that for such terms the world, not our practices, fixes the extension.

*Reply.* Tracking a kind is one function among others, the tracking function of §3.2, and a concept whose *only* function were to track a kind would indeed have its extension fixed by the kind. But "knowledge" has licensing functions that no kind can settle. Suppose there is a natural kind of reliably produced true belief, and suppose Kornblith is right that it is what the concept of knowledge tracks. The question whether *that* kind should govern the norm of assertion, or the norm of action under high stakes, is not answered by the kind; it is a question about assertion and action. Natural-kind realism about knowledge is therefore compatible with the account and does not threaten it; it identifies one function and says something true about it.

The point is confirmed by what happens to natural-kind terms themselves when they acquire further functions. "Fish" tracks a folk kind and, since the rise of cladistics, is also expected to track a clade; whales are the divergence case, and the resolution was a division of labour between folk and scientific vocabulary. "Species" is expected to track reproductive isolation, morphological cluster, ecological niche, and phylogenetic lineage, and these coincide for most vertebrates and diverge for asexual organisms and ring species; pluralism about species (Ereshefsky 1998) is disentanglement. "Gene" was expected to track a unit of inheritance, a unit of recombination, and a unit of function, and molecular biology separated them (Griffiths and Stotz 2013). "Planet" is the case already discussed. In each, a natural-kind term with several functions generated the structure of Chapter 4, and it was resolved by legislation where a practice had authority. Natural-kind realism, far from being a counterexample, supplies further instances.

**Normative realism.** The third form of the objection holds that there is a fact about what the practice of blame should require, and that the account, by calling the residue "normative", has not shown that it lacks an answer.

*Reply.* This is not an objection but a welcome addition, and §5.7 has already accepted it. If normative realism is true, the residual question of each persistent aporia has an answer, the answer is normative, and philosophy's disputes are objective disputes about it. The account denies only that the answer is a *descriptive* fact about the concept, supplied by the concept's content. It does not deny that there is an answer.

One further remark. The realist may say: "but surely Smith does not know; that is a fact, not a decision". Agreed. In Smith's case nearly all the concept's functions deliver the same verdict, and the verdict is as determinate as any verdict in the coincidence range. The account does not say that everything in the divergence region is undetermined. It says that verdicts there are function-relative, and that where the functions overwhelmingly agree the relativity is idle. Only in balanced cases is there a residue, and it is in balanced cases, and only there, that the disputes persist.

## 7.4 Conventionalism

*Objection.* If the residue is a matter of decision, then philosophy's persistent questions are settled by convention, like the side of the road on which one drives, and it is a matter of indifference how they are settled.

*Reply.* A convention, in the sense of Lewis (1969), coordinates where any of several options would serve equally well, so that what matters is only that everyone chooses the same one. The residual questions are not like this. The options differ in value: a concept of responsibility that tracks leeway and one that tracks reasons-responsiveness do different things to the practice of blame, and the difference matters to those blamed. The decision is therefore not conventional but *evaluative*, and evaluative decisions have better and worse answers, constrained by the ends of the practices as §5.7 argued. The Supreme Court's decision that a tomato is a vegetable was not arbitrary; it was reasoned from the purpose of the tariff schedule.

Moreover, the decision is the smaller part of the work. The location of the divergence region, the articulation of the functions, and the exhibition of the costs of privileging each are not decisions at all but discoveries, and they are the larger part of what the record of §6.3 contains. The account is a conventionalism about nothing except the final weighting, and about that it is not a conventionalism but a form of normative pluralism.

The account should be distinguished from Carnap's. Carnap (1950b) held that external questions are practical and, for that reason, not theoretical; he took "practical" to mean not truth-apt. The account holds that the residual questions are practical and *therefore* normative, and it is neutral on whether normative questions are truth-apt, which is a question in metaethics. Carnap's framework choice was, for him, a matter of expedience; the account's weighting is a matter of what the practices are for.

## 7.5 Circularity

*Objection.* The account is stated using the concepts of function, practice, need, and concept, and these are themselves polyfunctional. The dispute between etiological and role accounts of function (Wright 1973; Cummins 1975) is a stable aporia of exactly the kind the account describes. The account therefore presupposes what it explains.

*Reply.* It does, and this is a mark of consistency rather than a defect. The account is not a foundationalist project; it does not claim to state the structure of philosophy's problems from a standpoint outside them. It uses "function" in the role sense, says why (§3.2), and defends the choice in the currency appropriate to a weighting: by asking what the notion is needed for and arguing that the role sense serves that need. That is exactly the procedure the account recommends for any polyfunctional concept in its divergence region. A theory of polyfunctional concepts that could be stated only in monofunctional ones would be in an odd position; a theory that is in the same condition as its objects and handles that condition by its own recommended method is in a good one.

## 7.6 Demotion

*Objection.* The account reduces philosophy to lexicography plus ethics: it maps the uses of words and then hands the interesting question to moral philosophy. This is a handmaiden's role, and it is not what the great philosophers thought they were doing.

*Reply.* Four things.

First, no other discipline maps the divergence regions of load-bearing concepts in advance of the world's arrival there, and §6.5 argued that this work has a practical value that does not depend on convergence. It is not lexicography. Lexicography records how words are used; the construction of divergence cases discovers how they *could* be used and what would follow.

Second, the work is hard, creative, and cumulative. The record of §6.3 is the work of the best minds of the tradition, and each entry required the construction of a case no one had thought of, the articulation of a function no one had named, and the demonstration that a thesis everyone had accepted was true of one function and not of another. That is not a handmaiden's work.

Third, the residue is philosophy's too. The ethics of blame, of assertion, of medical decision, of legal liability, is moral and political philosophy. To say that the compatibilism debate is, in its residue, the ethics of blame is not to hand it to another discipline but to another part of the same one, and to say which part.

Fourth, and most importantly, the account restores rather than demotes philosophy's oldest self-understanding. Socrates did not tell Cephalus what justice is; he showed him where his concept ran out. Aristotle's method, stated at the opening of the discussion of *akrasia*, is to set out the appearances and the reputable opinions, to work through the puzzles they generate, and to preserve, if possible, all of them, or failing that the most and the most authoritative (*Nicomachean Ethics* VII.1, 1145b2–7). That is the aporetic method with an explicit prioritisation rule. His recurrent observation that "being", "good", and "cause" are "said in many ways" (*Metaphysics* Γ.2, 1003a33; *Nicomachean Ethics* I.6, 1096a23) is the recognition of polyfunctionality, and his practice of resolving puzzles by distinguishing senses is disentanglement. The account is not a reduction of philosophy to something less than it thought it was. It is a description of what it has, at its best, always done.

The image of the handmaiden also assumes that the sciences do the real work and philosophy tidies after them. The cases of §7.3 show otherwise. The sciences generate their own divergence cases, for "planet", "species", "gene", "mass", and "death", and when they do they need exactly the work the account describes, and do it badly without philosophy.

## 7.7 Success stories

*Objection.* Philosophy has converged on some things: on Turing's analysis of computability, on Frege's logic, on Tarski's treatment of truth in formalised languages, on the rejection of justified true belief. If the account were right, convergence should not happen. Since it does, the account is wrong, or at least incomplete.

*Reply.* The account predicts convergence in exactly three circumstances, and the success stories fall under them.

**Coincidence over the extended range.** Sometimes a concept's functions coincide not only in the environment of acquisition but over every case anyone can construct. "Effectively computable" is the paradigm. Turing (1936), Church (1936), and, by other routes, Gödel, Herbrand, Kleene, and Post gave formalisations that turned out to be provably equivalent, and the equivalence is what convinced the community that the informal concept had been captured (Church's thesis). The concept had several functions, what a clerk could do by rote, what a machine could do, what could be defined by recursion, and they coincided over the whole range. Explication was therefore unique up to equivalence, and the community converged. The account predicts convergence here, and predicts that it will take the form it took: multiple independent explications, discovered to agree.

**Authority of a single practice.** Sometimes one practice has authority over the concept and legislates. Set theory is the paradigm. Russell's paradox was a divergence case for "set" (§4.6), and the iterative conception (Boolos 1971) was a disentanglement; but what produced *convergence* on ZFC was that the mathematical practice's function, fruitfulness for classical mathematics together with apparent consistency, dominated, and the community accepted the legislation. Alternative set theories persist at the margins as a reminder that it was legislation, and philosophy's remaining set-theoretic disputes, about the justification of the iterative conception and about absolute generality, are the divergence region where mathematics does not need an answer. Tarski's treatment of truth is similar: by confining the definition to formalised languages and separating object language from metalanguage, Tarski legislated for the practice of formal semantics, which then converged, while explicitly declining to legislate for the philosophical dispute (1944, §§14–20).

**Decomposition.** Sometimes convergence is convergence on a component of disentanglement: that a case exists, that it separates two functions, that a thesis is true of one and not the other. The rejection of justified true belief is of this kind. Philosophers converged on the claim that Gettier's case separates justification from whatever knowledge requires, which is component (b) of §6.2, and did not converge on what to put in its place. The account predicts exactly this.

The success stories therefore confirm the account. Philosophy converges where the functions of a concept coincide over the extended range, where one practice has the authority to legislate, and on the components of disentanglement; and it does not converge on the headline question of a polyfunctional concept whose practices have no common authority. Austin's image of the sun throwing off sciences (1956) is the image of the first two cases: a concept's functions collapse into one, or one practice takes the concept over, and the question leaves philosophy because it has stopped being aporetic.

## 7.8 What the account does not cover

The account is a theory of persistent conceptual aporiai in the sense of §2.1, and I have tried throughout to mark its edges. Here I collect them.

**Factual metaphysical questions.** Whether God exists, whether there are abstract objects, whether the universe had a beginning: the parties to these debates take themselves to be disagreeing about how things are, and the account does not say they are wrong. It has something to say about existence questions where a distinction between existing and being fundamental has been drawn (§4.6), but the class as a whole lies outside it.

**Questions with a metaphysical residue.** In some aporiai the decomposition leaves a residue that is metaphysical rather than normative, and here the account explains the structure of the disagreement but not its content. The problem of consciousness is the clearest case. Chalmers's (1995) distinction between the easy problems and the hard problem is a disentanglement of the functions of "explain", mechanistic and predictive explanation on one side and something like rendering intelligible on the other, and the account predicts that verdicts on zombie and knowledge-argument cases will track which function of "explain" is salient. But the residual question, whether there is a further fact of phenomenal experience over and above the functional facts, is not a question about what a practice should require; it is a question about what there is, and it has the shape of the further-fact dispute of §7.3 rather than of the ethics of blame. The account says that the dispute has this shape. It does not say who is right. The same holds for the debate between A-theorists and B-theorists of time, which McTaggart (1908) initiated by disentangling two functions of temporal vocabulary and which has a metaphysical residue, and for external-world scepticism beyond the point where the certainty function of "knows" has been demoted.

**Formal and mathematical questions.** Questions settled by proof are not persistent, and the account has nothing to add to the epistemology of proof.

**Questions of interpretation and history.** These may be permanently contested but do not present aporetically.

**The problem of induction.** I am uncertain whether this falls inside or outside. "Justified", applied to inductive inference, has a function of licensing inference and a function of guaranteeing truth, and Hume's argument decorrelates them; Strawson's (1952, ch. 9) dissolution and Reichenbach's (1938) pragmatic vindication are prioritisations of the licensing function. So far the fit is good. But the residue, whether nature is uniform, is not obviously normative, and I leave the case open.

I regard candour about these limits as a strength. An account of philosophy's problems that claimed to cover everything philosophers have ever disputed would be suspect on that ground alone.

## 7.9 What would refute it

The account makes the following predictions, each of which could fail.

**(P1)** There is no persistent conceptual aporia, in the sense of §2.1, whose theses fail to map onto distinct functions of the central concept by the case-partition criterion. The discovery of an aporia around a demonstrably monofunctional concept would refute T1.

**(P2)** Competent users do not stably disagree about verdicts in a concept's coincidence range. Stable, informed, unforced disagreement about whether an ordinary perceiver in good conditions knows what she sees, or whether an ordinary agent acting on her reasons is responsible, would refute (3.4).

**(P3)** No headline question of a persistent aporia has been or will be settled by convergence without either decomposition or legislation by an authoritative practice. A case of convergence on such a question by discovery alone would refute T4.

**(P4)** Framing effects on verdicts in divergence cases track the salience of the concept's functions, not arbitrary features of presentation. Experimental findings that verdicts in balanced divergence cases vary with features unrelated to any function of the concept, and do not vary with manipulations of function salience, would refute (3.6).

**(P5)** No disentanglement is reversed. Since the case-partition criterion entails that functions once separated cannot be re-merged, the discovery of a case that re-merged them would show the criterion to be wrong.

Two empirical studies would test the account directly. The first is a *decomposed survey*: present professional philosophers with the headline question of an aporia and with its decomposed components, and predict a stable split on the headline together with near-consensus on the components. For free will the prediction is that respondents will divide on compatibilism in roughly the proportions of the PhilPapers surveys while agreeing, at rates above ninety per cent, that Frankfurt cases separate leeway from guidance control, that reasons-responsiveness is compatible with determinism, and that leeway in the unconditional sense is not. The second is a *salience manipulation*: present the same divergence case under framings that foreground different practices, a courtroom frame and a laboratory frame for a causal-selection case, a first-person and a third-person frame for a personal-identity case, and predict that verdicts shift in the direction of the function the frame engages. Williams (1970) and Nichols and Knobe (2007) have in effect already run versions of the second, and the results are as predicted; a systematic programme would put the account to a sharper test.
# Chapter 8. Conclusion: What Philosophy Is For

## 8.1 The argument in retrospect

I began with Cephalus, who knew what justice was until Socrates showed him a case in which the two things his concept did came apart. I have argued that this is the form of every persistent problem in philosophy, and that seeing this changes what we should think about philosophy's disagreements, its progress, and its point.

The argument had five steps. The concepts at the centre of philosophy's persistent problems are polyfunctional: each does several jobs in our practices, each job imposes its own application condition, and the conditions coincide across the range of cases from which the concept is learned (T1). The problems arise where the conditions come apart, and the thought experiment is the instrument by which such cases are constructed in advance of the world's producing them; this explains why each thesis of an aporetic cluster is compelling, why exotic cases are indispensable, and why the literature accumulates distinctions while the headline questions stand (T2). The positions in a persistent debate are prioritisations of functions, and what remains of the dispute once its factual and verbal layers are removed is a normative question about which function the shared concept should serve, a question that is rationally stable because nothing in the concept fixes the weights, the ends are plural, and no practice has authority over the rest (T3). Progress consists in locating divergence, articulating functions, disentangling them, and relocating the residue to the ethics of the relevant practice; this progress is cumulative, agreed across doctrinal divides, and constitutes understanding, and convergence on the headline is not to be expected (T4). The concept of progress is itself polyfunctional, and the metaphilosophical debate is a divergence case that the account predicts (T5).

The account was defended abductively, as the best explanation of eight features of philosophy's problems, through four detailed case studies and several sketched ones, and against objections from triviality, realism, conventionalism, circularity, demotion, and the apparent counterexamples of philosophy's convergent successes. It was shown to have limits, and to make predictions that could fail.

## 8.2 Consequences for method

If the account is right, several things follow about how philosophy should be done.

**Thought experiments are vindicated, and reinterpreted.** The recent scepticism about the case method (Cappelen 2012; Deutsch 2015; Machery 2017) and the recent defences of it (Nagel 2012; Williamson 2007) have shared an assumption: that the verdict elicited by a case is evidence about the extension of a single concept, to be trusted or distrusted as such. On the present account the verdict is a weighting, and the case is a device for discovering how many functions a concept has and where they diverge. So understood, the method is not merely defensible but indispensable: there is no other way to map a divergence region. But the verdicts must be read as what they are. A verdict on a balanced divergence case is not data about "the concept"; it is data about which of the concept's functions the case and its framing have made salient, and about the weighting of the person who gives it. The correct use of a divergence case is to map, not to test a unitary analysis.

**Conceptual analysis is reconceived as function-mapping.** The traditional project of finding necessary and sufficient conditions for a concept's application presupposes that there is one application condition to find. For polyfunctional concepts there is not, beyond the coincidence range, and the project's history of failure is explained. What replaces it is the mapping of a concept's functions, their application conditions, their coincidence range, and their divergence region, which is what Strawson (1992) called connective analysis and what the record of §6.3 in fact contains.

**Experimental philosophy is reconceived as the measurement of weightings.** The "restrictionist" reading of experimental findings, on which framing and demographic effects discredit the case method, and the defensive reading, on which the effects are noise to be filtered, both mistake what the effects are. They are measurements of function salience and of the distribution of weightings across a population, and as such they are data about the practices the concept serves. The finding that abstract framing elicits incompatibilist verdicts and concrete framing compatibilist ones is not an embarrassment to be explained away but a discovery about the functional structure of "responsible". A research programme that treated it so would ask which frames engage which functions, how weightings vary with the practices a population participates in, and whether professional training shifts them.

**Conceptual engineering is recognised as the explicit form of what philosophy has always done in the residue.** The recent literature on conceptual engineering (Cappelen 2018; Burgess, Cappelen, and Plunkett 2020) has been received as a departure from the tradition of analysis. On the present account it is the tradition become self-aware. Every doctrinal position in a persistent aporia was already a proposal for how a shared concept should behave in its divergence region; conceptual engineering says so openly, and asks what constraints such proposals should meet. The account supplies one constraint: an engineering proposal must be made with the map in hand, since a redesign that ignores a function the concept serves will wreck the practice that needed it.

**Pedagogy.** Persistent problems are usually taught as puzzles with a hidden answer that the student is invited to find. They should be taught as maps: here are the functions the concept serves, here are the cases that separate them, here are the costs of privileging each, and here is the question that remains once the map is drawn. A student who has learned that the compatibilism debate is, in its residue, a question about what blame is for has learned something true and has been placed in a position to think about it. A student who has learned that compatibilism is a thesis awaiting proof has been misled.

## 8.3 Consequences for the discipline's self-image

Kant's image of the battlefield, and the scientistic envy that has accompanied analytic philosophy since Russell, both rest on a comparison: philosophy is measured against inquiries whose questions have a different structure, and found wanting. The account replaces the comparison with a description. Philosophy is the discipline that maps, and where it can, legislates for, the divergence regions of the concepts that several of our practices load at once. It does this in advance of need, by constructing the cases the world has not yet produced; it does it cumulatively, by individuating functions that stay individuated; and it does it without the authority to impose a weighting, because the practices it serves have no common sovereign. Its persistent disagreements are the shadow of that last fact, and they are the price of serving plural practices honestly rather than legislating for one.

This is not a diminished self-image. It is, I have argued, the one that Socrates and Aristotle held, and it is the one the record of the discipline's actual achievements supports. The tradition's great results, from sense and reference to production and dependence, have the form the account describes, and the tradition's great unsettled questions have the form it predicts. A discipline that has done what philosophy has done does not need to apologise for not having done something its questions did not permit.

## 8.4 Future work

The account opens several lines of inquiry, which I list in order of the confidence with which I expect them to be fruitful.

**An empirical programme.** The decomposed survey and the salience manipulation of §7.9 are the most direct tests. A broader programme would measure the functional load of concepts, by counting the functions separable by cases and the practices they serve, and test the predicted correlation with the persistence and depth of the corresponding philosophical problems.

**A formal model.** The notions of §3.5 admit of formalisation: a concept as a bundle of application conditions over a space of cases, with a correlation structure over the space reflecting the environment of acquisition; the coincidence range and divergence region as regions of that space; functional load as a property of the bundle. Such a model would allow the account's predictions to be stated precisely and would connect it with formal work on learning from correlated features.

**A historical programme.** The history of philosophy could be reread as the history of conceptual fission: Aristotle's *pollachōs legetai*, the scholastic distinctions, the early modern separation of primary from secondary qualities, the Kantian separation of the analytic from the synthetic and the a priori from the a posteriori, and the twentieth century's record. The account predicts that each major disentanglement will have been forced by a divergence case and will have been followed by a stable dispute over the residue, and the history could be checked against the prediction.

**Applied work.** Machine learning is producing divergence cases for "agent", "author", "decision", "responsible", "understand", and "know" faster than any technology before it, and the practices that load these concepts, law, science, education, and ordinary life, have begun to legislate. The account says what the legislation requires: a map of each concept's functions and of what each proposed weighting would cost. Drawing those maps is work philosophy is placed to do and is not yet doing systematically.

**Metaethics.** The account is neutral on the status of the normative residue. Whether normative questions have objective answers determines whether philosophy's persistent disputes have them, and the account gives metaethics a new reason to matter: it is the theory of the residue.

## 8.5 Coda

Socrates did not tell Cephalus what justice is. He showed him where his concept ran out, and thereby gave him a choice he had not known he had, and a reason to think about what he wanted justice for. Cephalus, the dialogue tells us, went off to attend to the sacrifices, leaving the argument to his son. It is easy to read this as the failure of the exchange. It is better read as its success. The old man had learned that a concept he had used without difficulty all his life had two voices, and that in the region where they disagreed the question was not what justice *is* but what it should be *for*. That is not a discovery about a word. It is the beginning of ethics.

Wittgenstein asked what the aim of philosophy was and answered: to show the fly the way out of the fly-bottle (1953, §309). The account offered here suggests an amendment. The bottle has several exits, one for each of the practices that brought the fly in; the philosopher's task is not to choose one for the fly but to show it that they are there, that they lead to different places, and that where it goes is a question about where it needs to be. That is not the end of philosophy. It is what philosophy, at its best, has always been.
